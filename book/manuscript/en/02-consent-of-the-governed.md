# Chapter Two · Who Authorizes Government?

*In the People's Name: Privilege, Revolution, and Unaccountable Power · English draft v0.3 · 17 September 2026*

## After independence, still asking for freedom

A petition dated 13 January 1777, addressed to the Massachusetts Bay legislature, brought the question raised by American independence back before Americans themselves. Its signatories, seeking freedom for enslaved people, included Lancaster Hill and Prince Hall. They claimed freedom as a natural right shared by humanity and invoked the principles Americans had used against British rule in support of their own appeal.[9](93-notes.md#ch02-c09)

The petition's force lay in a demand: apply what you have already told the world to our condition as well. If consent is the basis of legitimate government, people living within the new country are also entitled to ask where they have given theirs.

The previous chapter examined laws that assigned status before birth. This chapter turns to another political proposition: government should receive its authority from those it governs. That proposition can unsettle hereditary power, but it also poses difficulties for successful resisters. Who gives authority, how are representatives selected, and who is excluded from the people entitled to authorize government?

To understand the petition of 1777, we must first return to colonial representatives who still spoke as British subjects. They did not begin by framing every demand in the language of founding a new country.

## Claiming the rights of subjects

The Stamp Act Congress's resolutions of 19 October 1765 opened with loyalty to the Crown while insisting that taxation required consent given personally or through representatives. They maintained that the colonies were not represented in the British House of Commons and that taxation there should proceed through their own elected representative bodies. The document still concluded by calling for loyal petitions to king and Parliament.[1](93-notes.md#ch02-c01)

Loyalty and a demand for rights appeared together. The delegates invoked the standing they believed they possessed as British subjects. Their argument was that living across the Atlantic did not entitle someone else within the empire to consent on their behalf to giving up their property.

Taxation made the question of authority concrete. A legislature can decide who must bear a cost, who must pay and what follows from failing to pay. The dispute therefore concerned who was entitled to decide and whether those bearing the burden could influence the decision through their own representatives. Even a tax that does not threaten financial ruin can raise a serious question about where that authority resides.

Why should taxpayers care who chooses their representatives? If those deciding a tax need not seek the support of those who pay it, they can demand payment under less pressure from their objections. If voters can replace representatives who support a tax, those representatives have reason to consider that cost before voting. Authorization thus concerns a practical relationship: must those making decisions answer to those bearing the consequences?

These delegates were still seeking that relationship within the empire. Their loyalty carried a demand for rights: as subjects, they should be entitled to participate through local representatives in deciding what they surrendered. That position placed the hope of redress in the existing order's capacity to recognize their standing.

## The empire gives another answer

Britain's Declaratory Act of 18 March 1766 asserted the authority of Crown and Parliament to legislate for the American colonies and their inhabitants in all matters. It declared colonial resolutions denying or questioning that authority null and void.[2](93-notes.md#ch02-c02)

The imperial position was expressed by Crown and Parliament together. Colonial representatives demanded consent to taxation through their own local institutions; the imperial authority maintained its supreme legislative competence. George III was part of that structure, but the power in dispute also included Parliament's power over the colonies. The two sides supplied different answers to who could make the final decision.

The disagreement thus extended beyond whether a particular measure was moderate. Even if a burden could be adjusted, similar conflicts could recur where rule-making power did not depend on local consent. Conversely, if colonial legislatures could reject certain decisions of the imperial Parliament, the scope of imperial authority would require a different account.

A deeper structure of political conflict appears here. One party understands a common order as requiring acceptance of a superior authority's final decision. Another understands a legitimate decision as requiring its own participation. Both can invoke law without recognizing the same relationship of authorization.

Scrutiny cannot therefore stop at the statement that a power has legal grounds. It must trace whose voice those grounds recognize and how the system treats people who reject them. Law can express and organize power while also becoming part of the dispute over power itself.

## From seeking redress to declaring separation

By the summer of 1776, the Continental Congress faced the question of independence. The independence resolution passed on 2 July; the text of the Declaration of Independence was adopted on 4 July after further revision.[3](93-notes.md#ch02-c03) Having decided to separate, the delegates also had to explain to the world why they were entitled to end the existing relationship of obedience.

The Declaration linked government's purpose to protecting rights, grounded its just powers in the consent of the governed, and asserted the people's right to alter or abolish government destructive of those purposes. It also cautioned against changing long-established governments for minor, temporary causes.[4](93-notes.md#ch02-c04)

The argument shifted the burden of justification. A ruler's historical standing could not, alone, settle the legitimacy of power. Protection of rights and a basis in consent became grounds for assessment. At the same time, resistance needed serious and continuing reasons; a passing grievance did not make the shared political order something to discard at will.

The Declaration then accused the king of dissolving representative bodies, making judges dependent on his will and imposing taxes without consent.[4](93-notes.md#ch02-c04) These charges directed attention to oversight and redress. If representatives could be dismissed and judges depended on the power under challenge, where could someone suffering an injury raise an effective objection?

Where an authority can determine the rules and constrain those reviewing its conduct, obtaining redress becomes especially difficult. Articulating that problem allows political argument to move beyond a ruler's kindness to the powers he holds and the limits upon them.

The independence claim consequently reached beyond replacing the person at the top. It asserted that government exists to secure rights and must explain the source of its authority. Once advanced, that principle could also be used to examine a new government. The victors could not demand consent only when opposing someone else's rule, then permanently close the question after acquiring power themselves.

For this principle to constrain the victors, people must retain powers they can actually use: choosing representatives, reviewing decisions and seeking redress when rights are violated. Otherwise, the Declaration may continue to be commemorated while people still have no way to refuse the demands of those in power.

## Keeping representatives accountable

John Adams offered an institutional proposal in his Thoughts on Government of April 1776. He advocated a representative legislature reflecting the people, warned against concentrating legislative, executive and judicial powers in one assembly, and called for an independent judiciary.[5](93-notes.md#ch02-c05)

The warning turned the principle of opposition to despotism toward representative institutions themselves. If the same people make rules, enforce them and adjudicate accusations against themselves, they may use one power to protect another. Being elected does not remove an interest in concealing mistakes or keeping office. Judicial independence matters because it gives an injured person a chance to bring a challenge somewhere the decision-maker cannot arbitrarily control.

Pennsylvania's constitution of 28 September 1776 provided another concrete arrangement. Representatives were to be elected annually and could serve no more than four years in seven. Electoral qualifications included free status, a minimum age of twenty-one, residence and tax requirements, with an exception to the tax condition for sons of freeholders meeting the age requirement.[6](93-notes.md#ch02-c06)

These provisions placed consent within recurring choices. Annual elections required representatives wishing to remain in office to seek eligible voters' support again, while tenure limits restricted how long they could continue occupying a seat. People without electoral qualifications could not exert the same pressure through that vote. Whom representatives had reason to answer to was thus directly connected to who could take part in choosing them.

Pennsylvania adopted a single legislative assembly, differing from Adams's proposal.[6](93-notes.md#ch02-c06) People in the same period could share opposition to despotism while giving different answers about how to constrain a representative body. That disagreement kept institutional choices open. It also requires us to examine separately whom each arrangement empowered to decide and to correct decisions.

Specific arrangements explain more than an abstract label. The interval between elections, who can vote, how long someone can hold office and who can review executive decisions all shape the relationship between representatives and those represented. A claim to come from the people has a chance of becoming a continuing constraint only when it enters such arrangements.

This also explains why authorization cannot be understood solely as support at the beginning. Someone may support establishing a government and oppose its later decisions. If all opposition is treated as betrayal of the original common cause, authorization risks becoming a commitment that can be given but never reconsidered.

## A letter asking that women be remembered

Before the Declaration was adopted, Abigail Adams had already put the question to her husband. On 31 March 1776 she asked John to remember women when making new laws, opposed unlimited authority for husbands, and questioned why women should be bound by laws in which they had neither voice nor representation. She added a continuation to the letter on 5 April.[7](93-notes.md#ch02-c07)

She brought the political problem of domination into the household. Opposition to the arbitrary power of a distant ruler could be connected, through the same principle, to limits on power close at hand. Valuing one's own liberty does not guarantee willingness to reconsider the authority one holds over others.

In his private reply of 14 April, John responded in a teasing register, saying that masculine systems would not be repealed and joking that men possessed little more than the name of masters.[8](93-notes.md#ch02-c08) Abigail's question about power met a familiar kind of answer: the existing relationship was not as troubling as its critic suggested.

But for someone at the other end of the relationship, limiting a husband's power, escaping cruel treatment and holding him accountable were practical questions of everyday life. Abigail's letter connected those demands to women's voice in law.[7](93-notes.md#ch02-c07) If those making the rules need not obtain the consent of those subjected to them, they may keep imposing an arrangement they consider mild upon people who do not find it mild at all.

The question she posed nevertheless has continuing force. Can institutions protecting liberty apply their own standard to familiar relationships? Does a form of domination cease to require justification because habit makes it appear natural?

This private letter preserved an objection, giving the historical “people” a voice beyond public assemblies. Before the new order was fully established, Abigail was already asking those making the rules to count her too. Liberalism must take that demand seriously: intimacy, habit and another person's professed kindness are insufficient grounds for denying anyone equal protection.

## Apply the common principle to us

Return now to the petition of January 1777. The petitioners sought legislation restoring freedom and asked that their children born in the country not remain enslaved after reaching twenty-one. The document challenged the inconsistency of opposing subjection for oneself while imposing it upon others.[9](93-notes.md#ch02-c09)

This was a request awaiting the legislature's response. The proposed threshold of twenty-one for children still left room for continued enslavement before that age. It also exposed a practical question confronting those seeking freedom: who could decide when freedom began? Even where the same natural right was acknowledged, leaving its timing to those in power could still place the cost of waiting on those subjected to them.

Lancaster Hill, Prince Hall and the other signatories placed their demands jointly before the legislature. They asked for freedom to be restored through law, making release from bondage a question of public rules. Reliance on individual acts of manumission still left those holding power to decide who could leave. Abolishing that domination through general rules would change the parties' original positions.

The petition turned the political language of independence into a demand addressed inward. It did more than ask those with power to be kinder. It demanded a consistent scope for a shared principle. If opposition to enforced subjection could justify independence, an explanation was needed for why a similar claim to freedom lost its force when made by another group.

The petitioners thereby made a public principle a basis for pressure on legislators. A refusal would confront legislators with the conflict between their grounds for independence and the domination before them. Whether that pressure could change the law also depended on how effectively the petition could be advanced and whether those defending slavery retained the power to prevent change.

The value of the independence claim also lay in how excluded people could use it. A promise supplied grounds for challenge. Effective protection required enabling someone to obtain freedom and to stop others from taking it away. Taking the promise that far was what treating it seriously demanded.

## Government must keep explaining itself

From the loyal petitioning of 1765 and the assertion of imperial legislative power in 1766 to independence and constitutional design in 1776, authorization emerged as a set of specific disputes: who could tax, who could legislate, who could elect representatives, and what would constrain those representatives once they had power.

Abigail's letter and the joint petition of 1777 pressed further. Someone could be included among those required to obey a government without being equally included among those entitled to determine it. Assessing whether people possess political power requires examining both boundaries.

The book therefore carries a standard into the histories that follow: those claiming to represent the people should permit scrutiny of that relationship, explain the limits of their authority, and preserve ways to correct and change it. Revolution, historical achievement and common ideals may all form part of a political narrative. They do not, alone, answer these institutional questions.

American independence connected legitimate governmental power with rights and consent. Giving that claim fuller effect still required people to struggle, legislators to decide, and those excluded to continue pressing their demands.

The next chapter examines slaveholders in a free republic: how interests in slavery conflicted with promises of liberty, and how abolition changed legally protected domination. Independence gave a country a new political justification. Whether that justification would reach into each person's life remained work to be done.
