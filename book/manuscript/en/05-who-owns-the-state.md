# Chapter Five · After the Revolution, Who Owns the State?

*Privilege and Freedom: Who Makes the Rules, Who Pays the Price · English draft v0.3 · 17 September 2026*

## The rice brought home

In the preface to *Tombstone*, Yang Jisheng recalled bringing rice home from school in late April 1959. His father, Yang Xiushen, could no longer swallow and died three days later. The man who had raised him was his paternal uncle. Yang wrote that he still believed in the Great Leap Forward at the time. He understood the death as a family misfortune, without knowing what was happening farther away.[1](93-notes.md#ch05-s01)

The rice had arrived, but it could not save a life. A family's struggle to survive was also a problem of understanding: someone could witness a catastrophe without yet knowing whom to question about its causes.

For Yang, the death before him remained disconnected from the cause in which he still believed. The population, labor, and food requirements in a state plan ultimately correspond to individuals who must eat, who can be hurt, and whose lives cannot be lived again. Liberalism begins here with a clear judgment: the grandeur of a cause cannot offset a person's life, and those making plans must answer to those bearing their consequences.

The previous chapter asked how workers could constrain an organization claiming to represent them. This chapter examines selected institutions and events after the Chinese Communist Party took power, following three connected forms of authority: who decides, who distributes the resources people need to live, and who can say that a decision is wrong.

To own the state, in this discussion, concerns effective control and accountability. A person need not hold legal title to state assets to use an office to determine what others receive. Someone proclaimed a master of the country may nevertheless be unable to make an official answer a question.

## Who defines the people?

A government claiming to represent the people must still answer who may oppose it. If rulers also control the definition of who belongs to the people, they can turn a dispute about policy into an examination of whether dissenters deserve protection.

In *On the People's Democratic Dictatorship*, dated 30 June 1949, Mao Zedong defined the people at that stage as workers, peasants, the urban petty bourgeoisie, and the national bourgeoisie. He distinguished them from the “reactionaries” subject to dictatorship. The working class would lead through the Communist Party, and stronger state power would create conditions for socialism, communism, and the eventual disappearance of the state.[2](93-notes.md#ch05-s02)

This carried forward the problem of transition examined in the last chapter. Class divisions and coercion would disappear in the future, but a government capable of coercion had to be established in the present. The people were both the source of power and a politically defined category. Membership among them, or classification as their enemy, carried different political consequences.

The question is therefore not only who supports a desirable goal. It is also who has the authority to classify others. If opposing a policy can place someone outside the protected category of the people, the first thing that person must defend is their standing to disagree at all.

The danger in this classification is that those in power may become both accuser and judge. Punishing crimes requires specific evidence and a procedure through which accusations can be challenged. The political label of enemy supplies neither. The more an organization can decide who loses protection, the more an individual may put avoiding exclusion ahead of maintaining an objection.

Revolutionary victory tells us who acquired governing power. It does not, by itself, answer whether people born afterward consent to that rule or whether earlier supporters can change their minds. Between historical achievement and continuing authorization lies a distance that institutions must bridge.

## The party decides; the government executes

Someone facing a mistaken decision will often first approach the official carrying it out. If the answer is that the instruction came from above, accountability must reach further up. Who holds power depends on how far that questioning can go and who can make it stop.

The Constitution adopted on 20 September 1954 placed all state power in the people, to be exercised through national and local people's congresses. It required state organs to accept public supervision and officials to obey the Constitution and law. It also provided for independent adjudication and citizens' rights to complain about unlawful conduct or dereliction of duty and to obtain compensation for infringements of their rights.[3](93-notes.md#ch05-s03)

Supervision, complaints, and compensation address precisely the situation in which an individual comes into conflict with the state. To constrain power, the person receiving a complaint must be able to investigate the decision-maker, and a remedy must remain effective when that decision-maker resists it.

A different relationship of authority appears in Mao's speech of 12 August 1953. He required major and important questions to be discussed and decided by party committees before the government carried out the decisions. He also emphasized collective leadership within the party, opposed the center taking over every minor matter, and required lower bodies to obey higher ones and the whole party to obey its Central Committee.[4](93-notes.md#ch05-s04)

Two paths of responsibility therefore need to be compared. Under the arrangements of state institutions, officials were to answer to representative bodies. Under party leadership, major decisions were first formed within party committees. An official implementing a decision could explain to the public that an instruction from above was being carried out. If people believed the instruction itself was wrong, what procedure allowed them to change the institution issuing it?

Collective discussion within a party can constrain some individual abuses, but who participates still matters. A decision shared by several leaders does not establish that affected people can select those leaders, organize an alternative, or initiate accountability. Collective leadership addresses relationships among leaders. Public accountability must also address relationships between leaders and those they govern.

These arrangements change the pressures on cadres, the personnel entrusted with organizational and administrative responsibilities. If an office gives someone power to distribute resources and impose penalties, while promotion and personal security depend on evaluation from above, disregarding local needs may cost less than disregarding a superior's demands. Even someone willing to speak for the public needs protection for refusing a mistaken instruction. Otherwise, serving the people can readily give way to satisfying superiors first.

## Who counts the grain, and who decides what remains?

For a grain producer, the urgent question is how much will remain after delivery. For a cadre responsible for procurement, it may be whether the quota from above can be met. Once these concerns conflict, the power to revise the figure becomes the power to leave someone else bearing the loss.

The order on planned grain procurement and supply, adopted on 19 November 1953, required producers with surplus grain to sell specified kinds and quantities at state-set prices. Quotas passed down through administrative levels, with village publication and public appraisal required. Private grain merchants could no longer trade independently, although they could sell on state commission. After tax and procurement obligations were met, remaining grain could still be stored, used, or sold through designated channels.[5](93-notes.md#ch05-s05)

The arrangement preserved uses for grain remaining after obligations were met, but the quota first determined how much could remain. Village publication and appraisal provided an opportunity to raise objections. If farmers could point out that a figure was false without securing a reassessment, publication alone could not protect their food supply.

Surplus grain sounds like a quantity already known. In fact, it contains a decision. Assessing what can be procured requires knowing the harvest, food needs, seed requirements, and other uses. An estimate that departs from reality can turn a surplus on paper into a shortage in people's lives.

If a quota rests on an inaccurate estimate of the harvest, reassessment forces superiors to confront the error in that estimate. Continuing to seek grain can instead leave the conflict in the village for the time being. If admitting error carries a political cost while farmers bear most of the cost of insisting on the quota, officials have an incentive to keep applying pressure. Farmers' appeals, officials' authority to suspend further demands, and outside investigation are constraints that can interrupt this process.

If producers are held responsible for meeting a target, those setting it must also answer for whether it is achievable. Otherwise, responsibility for an error can pass downward. Superiors do not revisit the estimate, subordinates continue looking for grain, and those unable to deliver it bear the final pressure.

## Leaving also requires permission

When someone can neither change an arrangement governing their life nor readily leave it, those in authority face one less pressure to respond. Difficulty in leaving can tilt an already unequal relationship further against the individual.

Article 90 of the 1954 Constitution provided for freedom of residence and movement. The household registration regulations promulgated on 9 January 1958, however, required a rural resident moving to a city to obtain one of three documents for the departure registration: proof of recruitment by an urban labor department, admission to a school, or permission from the city's household registration authority. The regulations also addressed temporary residence, extensions, and migration conditions.[6](93-notes.md#ch05-s06)

These conditions concerned migration and settlement procedures, rather than every journey. For a rural resident hoping to live in a city, finding somewhere else to go was insufficient: the required documentation also had to be obtained. The opportunity to change one's life therefore depended in part on those able to approve the move.

Demanding a change and leaving an arrangement are capacities that both need protection. An ability to leave should not be used to insist that people abandon their homes and their claims to accountability. An ability to apply does not mean that they are free to go. Refusal requires reasons and a procedure for overturning mistaken decisions, while an applicant's livelihood cannot be put on hold during an appeal.

Those controlling resources and those controlling permission to move could therefore both affect a family's ability to escape hardship. If requests for help met no response while leaving remained difficult, the family would depend more heavily on those in power acknowledging a problem. How information traveled upward became part of survival itself.

## The person raising the problem becomes the problem

On 14 July 1959, Peng Dehuai wrote to Mao about problems in the Great Leap Forward, the drive for rapid increases in production. In notes written during and after the Lushan Conference, he explained that the letter drew on correspondence, internal materials, and discussions. He identified excessive speed, exaggeration, and damage to agriculture. He still supported the direction of the Great Leap and the communes. These were an internal supporter's demands for adjustment.[7](93-notes.md#ch05-s07)

Peng accepted the basic goal yet faced political risk for identifying problems in its execution. Other cadres therefore had to weigh two things: whether a report was true and whether reporting it might make them appear disloyal. Once policy results became a test of loyalty, honest reporting could endanger the reporter's place in the organization.

The party's historical resolution, adopted in 1981, subsequently acknowledged that the criticism of Peng late in the Lushan Conference and the following campaign against right deviation had been wrong. It stated that they damaged democracy within the party and interrupted the correction of Great Leap errors, prolonging them.[8](93-notes.md#ch05-s08)

Punishing a critic can affect people far beyond the person punished. Others learn which statements may be dangerous and decide whether next time to report, omit, soften, or remain silent. The resolution's acknowledgment that correction was interrupted also provides support for understanding this loss of information.

A system need not make everyone speak identically to lose access to accurate information. If bringing bad news carries a risk of punishment without corresponding protection, people must consider the cost of reporting it. What superiors hear may become increasingly consistent while moving farther from reality.

The highest leader need not personally handle every village for punishment of criticism to change the information each level is willing to send upward. Subordinates also bear their own responsibility for false reports and coercive acts. Responsibility must be traced through decisions, reports, and penalties, because suppressing bad news itself increases the danger of the next mistaken decision.

## When a request for food becomes a political offense

In his investigation of Xinyang in *Tombstone*, Yang Jisheng described Li Ruiying leading a work team to Jigong Mountain in 1959. The team found hunger where exemplary crop yields were supposed to have been achieved. She requested grain from Xinyang party secretary Lu Xianwen and was accused of right deviation. Yang placed the episode within his account of inflated yields, excessive procurement, and the suppression of disagreement.[9](93-notes.md#ch05-s09)

Yang's Xinyang investigation drew on interviews and documentary material, with a different basis of knowledge from his recollection of his father. It presents another predicament: Li had already brought hunger to the leadership's attention, only to have to defend her political standing to make that report. For those waiting for grain, the channel for seeking help could become another obstacle.

If reporting hunger is interpreted as denying an achievement, investigation is turned around. Instead of establishing how much grain remains, attention shifts to identifying who doubts the official success. Assigning a political label to the reporter cannot change the absence of food.

Privilege here need not begin with luxurious living. It can take the form of a position insulated from correction. Decision-makers define success and can punish people bringing contrary information, while those bearing the consequences cannot demand equally searching scrutiny of the decision-makers.

Li's request for grain also reveals differences of judgment among cadres. Those willing to correct errors need protection beyond a superior's personal preferences if their help is to endure. Otherwise, their ability to acknowledge a disaster remains dependent on support higher in the hierarchy, and the fate of those seeking help changes with that support.

A food crisis demands timely decisions. A later reassessment may restore someone's reputation, but it cannot send food back into days already past. Effective accountability must be possible before the damage spreads.

## Who protects a teacher's life?

In a 2014 statement, Bian Zhongyun's husband, Wang Jingyao, recounted that student Red Guards had beaten his wife to death at the Girls' Middle School attached to Beijing Normal University on 5 August 1966. He called for the truth to be established. The Blinken Open Society Archives at Central European University holds a catalog record for *Though I Am Gone* that quotes director Hu Jie describing Wang's photographs of his wife after her death and their preservation.[10](93-notes.md#ch05-s10)

This was, first, the death of a person. Even within a vast political movement, Bian's experience cannot be reduced to an illustration of a struggle between political lines. Her name and the date of her death remind us that this was no abstract cost to be passed over.

Preserving a record does not automatically establish truth or responsibility. The record needs protection, examination, and a process capable of reaching judgments. If even asking questions must wait for a change in the political climate, the victim's family still lacks a remedy it can use on its own initiative.

Personal safety must not depend on whether a teacher receives a favorable political assessment. Criticism of someone's views, or a belief that the person should lose a post, gives no one a right to beat them. Criticism, dismissal, investigation, and criminal punishment have different requirements. Collective anger cannot replace all of them at once.

Wang left a bereaved husband's account and preserved records, rather than testimony that he had witnessed the entire campus assault. Each participant's responsibility still requires separate investigation. Protection for someone under attack, however, cannot wait until all responsibility has been established afterward. Those stopping violence must have authority to say no without first proving that the victim was on the right political side.

## Does mobilizing the people put them in control?

Adopted on 8 August 1966, three days after Bian's death, the Cultural Revolution's Sixteen Points called for the masses to emancipate themselves while placing the relevant mass organizations under Communist Party leadership. It proposed elections, criticism, and the replacement of representatives. In its discussion of contradictions among the people, it also required protection of differing views and persuasion instead of coercion or force.[11](93-notes.md#ch05-s11)

This later document does not establish the specific orders preceding Bian's death. It does express a tension within the movement: expanding mobilization while requiring restraint. If stopping violence can be interpreted as suppressing revolutionary enthusiasm, while tolerating it brings less accountability, restraint becomes hard to sustain. Persuasion and protection for dissent need institutions capable of enforcement before they become boundaries on which victims can rely.

The distinction between mass mobilization and public control becomes clearer here. People may be permitted to criticize certain cadres and even force them from office. It does not follow that they can also reject the decision to launch the movement, or require its highest leader to stop it and submit to scrutiny.

Intensity of political participation and security of individual rights are different measures. A crowd may be forceful while an individual lacks room to disagree safely. Requiring everyone to declare a position does not give everyone a right to refuse participation.

Cadres under attack can themselves become victims in such relationships. Holding an office that once gave them power does not mean they can always withstand higher authority or pressure from a movement. Privilege is therefore not a simple label that makes certain families permanently secure or their members permanently united. We need to examine who can command or punish across levels of authority, and whether rules protect individuals.

Opposition to arbitrary harm must protect the accused as well as the admired. Otherwise, overcoming privilege can become little more than taking turns deciding who will be left unprotected.

## After admitting error, who receives the power?

The 1981 historical resolution identified the Cultural Revolution of 1966–76 as a grave error initiated and led by Mao and rejected its principal propositions. It also described assaults on party organizations and the persecution of cadres and others. At the same time, it maintained the authority of Mao Zedong Thought and the party's historical legitimacy.[8](93-notes.md#ch05-s08)

An organization that has suffered grave failure also has reasons to reassess its past: restoring its operations, recovering trust, and reassuring members that the same dangers will not recur. Acknowledging errors can serve these needs while opening space to restore victims' reputations. Both effects can coexist. What matters is whether victims can keep asking questions beyond the organization's conclusions.

If the organization facing accountability still determines the scope and final conclusions of an investigation, correction may stop at the limits compatible with preserving its legitimacy. Voluntarily admitting some errors imposes a different constraint from having to answer unwelcome questions. Victims and independent investigators need the power to require those answers.

In his speech of 18 August 1980 on reforming party and state leadership, Deng Xiaoping had already identified bureaucracy, excessive concentration of power, patriarchal practices, lifetime tenure in leading posts, and privileges. He connected errors to organizational arrangements and specifically described cadres using their positions to help relatives and friends move to cities, obtain jobs, or secure promotion.[12](93-notes.md#ch05-s12)

Such privilege requires no noble title. When an office can turn public decisions into special opportunities for friends and relatives, political authority can shape the distribution of opportunity. The problem extends beyond an individual's appetite. Who can find out, who can object, and who can deprive someone abusing an office of the ability to continue?

The same speech called for public supervision and lawful removal of those abusing privilege, and proposed that the party's Central Committee submit changes in state leadership to the state's representative institutions. It placed reform within continued socialism and party leadership.[12](93-notes.md#ch05-s12) Criticism of concentrated power and preservation of the party's leading role thus coexisted within the proposal. Promising changes to personnel and institutions did not directly answer whether the public could replace the governing organization.

Replacing particular cadres can stop some abuses without changing the rewards and penalties facing their successors. If superiors still control careers and victims still struggle to initiate an investigation, obedience and concealment may remain more advantageous than correction. Institutional reform must alter these pressures if individual responsibility is to mean more than a moral judgment made afterward.

## The people cannot be owners in name alone

This chapter has located state power in places that can be examined: how meetings decide policies, how grain leaves its producers, which documents a move requires, how reports of difficulty are received, and who can question an assault on a person.

Together, these relationships expose a problem. If the people supply labor, obey arrangements, and bear the consequences but cannot effectively scrutinize resources, challenge classifications, or change decision-makers, a state said to belong to them lacks the conditions for them to act as its governing public.

Public ownership must mean more than the name assigned to resources, and popular sovereignty more than the title given to institutions. They need to take the form of usable capacities: learning the grounds for decisions, joining with others to express dissent, demanding an end to harm, establishing particular responsibilities, and replacing representatives when necessary.

A state's ability to carry out a long-term plan is one capacity. People's ability to stop a mistaken plan from continuing to hurt them is another. Assessing a state's achievements cannot consist of counting the first while treating the second as an obstruction.

The rice in the opening account did not save Yang Xiushen. The writing that survived allows later readers to place an individual's experience within a public inquiry. Memory must ultimately serve rights that living people can exercise. Each generation should not have to suffer a disaster and wait for the next to restore its reputation.

The next chapter, *Open Markets and Political Continuity*, examines reform, economic choice, and political authority. Did greater room for markets also enlarge the individual's ability to constrain the state? Which criticisms of concentrated power became enforceable limits, and which remained dependent on leaders' judgment?

Removing old rulers did not make the new rulers naturally belong to the people. That relationship must be tested from the other direction: can people require them to stop, make them answer, and choose someone else when they no longer deserve trust?
