# Chapter Nine　How Interests Bind and Diverge

*Privilege and Freedom: Who Makes the Rules, Who Pays the Price · English draft v0.2 · 17 September 2026*

## Benefits before shared beliefs

Hong Kong leaves us with a question: why can people with different objectives sustain a relationship? One participant seeks profit, another wants access to power, and a third hopes to reduce outside scrutiny. They need not endorse one another's entire outlook. As long as an exchange remains worthwhile, they have a reason to continue. Understanding their cooperation begins with what each contributes and receives—and who ultimately bears the cost.

In 2025, two people working for the Chinese chemical company AMARVEL BIOTECH received lengthy prison sentences in the United States: twenty-five years for Qingzhou Wang and fifteen for Yiyi Chen. Their convictions included a precursor-importation conspiracy and a money-laundering conspiracy. On special questions concerning the precursor conspiracy, the jury found that both knew or had reasonable cause to believe the chemicals would be used to manufacture fentanyl. It did not find the manufacturing intent addressed in a separate question. Both were acquitted on another count alleging a conspiracy involving fentanyl and related substances.[1](93-notes.md#ch09-i01)

The verdict placed responsibility on particular conduct and states of knowledge. Precursors are materials used in further manufacture; many stages may separate a supplier from the person who ultimately consumes a drug. A supplier need not personally make the final sale to be responsible for joining a criminal transaction. Purpose, participation, and financial arrangements can all matter in establishing that responsibility.

The Justice Department's account of the trial evidence also records that undercover buyers explicitly told the defendants the purchases were intended for fentanyl production.[1](93-notes.md#ch09-i01) The transaction conveyed a demand upstream: buyers explained what they wanted to accomplish, suppliers offered goods and services, and revenue made continued participation attractive.

Describing that choice as profit-seeking does not diminish its wrongfulness. Someone who knows, or has sufficient reason to recognize, that a transaction harms others cannot use a division of labor to excuse putting income above human life. The question is how a network can reward each participating link while excluding those exposed to harm from every relevant decision.

## Borders do not end the division of labor

A separate case places Canada on a specific distribution route. In a July 2023 sentencing announcement, the Justice Department described how Canadian national Jason Joey Berry coordinated the acquisition of fentanyl and analogues from China, receipt in Canada, and distribution to the United States. He received a twenty-four-year prison sentence. The announcement also identified Canadian law enforcement's assistance with the investigation.[2](93-notes.md#ch09-i02)

This and AMARVEL are distinct cases. They reveal different positions within cross-border crime: upstream supply can be separated from downstream distribution, while intermediaries seek opportunities across jurisdictions. Participants need not share political convictions to cooperate around continuing illegal income.

A division of labor creates a dangerous distance. Each participant may describe their own role as limited, while the person harmed cannot easily discover whose hands a transaction passed through. The longer the chain, the more an investigation must reconnect communications, deliveries, and money within a particular operation. Borders add coordination work for investigators and may offer criminals obstacles behind which to evade accountability.

Canadian police assistance belongs in Berry's story too. Traffickers operating in Canada and Canadian officers investigating them took opposing actions. Compressing both into an undifferentiated “Canada” obscures whom a criminal network actually depends on, as well as which forms of cooperation can disrupt it.

Criticism becomes stronger when it identifies those differences. People who supply drugs to the United States, profit from the trade, facilitate it, or investigate it should answer for their own actions or receive appropriate recognition. If nationality substitutes for responsibility, actual participants can disappear inside an oversized label.

## Victims on both sides

In data updated in June 2026, the Public Health Agency of Canada recorded approximately 5,600 apparent opioid toxicity deaths for 2025. The corresponding substance-specific statistics put fentanyl involvement at 56%, with coverage that was not identical to the national total. The most recent four quarters remained subject to revision, and some jurisdictions used broader reporting definitions. The total cannot be treated as a count of deaths all caused by fentanyl.[3](93-notes.md#ch09-i03)

These figures establish serious harm within Canada as well. The suffering of Americans does not depend on Canadians having escaped it. A cross-border network can supply the United States while exposing people in Canada to danger. Profit does not automatically protect those who share a participant's nationality.

When public debate treats deaths only as ammunition in a dispute between countries, individuals are excluded again. Families lose members, communities require treatment and continuing support, and public institutions bear the costs of investigation and response. Those burdens remain after everyone along a supply chain has completed their transaction.

The scale of the Canadian route also requires dates and defined measures. A comparison on page 22 of the DEA's 2025 assessment reported 22.7 kilograms of Canadian-sourced fentanyl seized at the US–Canada border in 2024, against 9,354 kilograms at the US–Mexico border. Seizures cannot be converted directly into actual flows or supply shares. The 2026 US National Drug Control Strategy subsequently identified Canadian production and trafficking into the United States as a growing threat.[4](93-notes.md#ch09-i04)

A historical difference in seizures can coexist with a growing risk. Taking the northern route seriously does not require declaring it the largest. Uncertainty about scale does not prevent investigation of organizations already identified. Public resources should follow risks that can be examined and reassessed, rather than the loudest description of them.

## Following responsibility upward

Tracing a network beyond businesses and traffickers brings regulatory and political power into view. What did the relevant authorities know, when did they know it, what powers did they possess, and what did they do? If someone obstructed an investigation after receiving a warning, protected a particular operator, or directed an operation, those decisions belong in the chain of responsibility.

An order bearing the highest leader's signature is not the only possible starting point. Independent communications, financial records, accounts of intervention, and testimony grounded in knowledge may together explain who controlled an operation. Their value lies in connecting a person to particular conduct, beyond showing capacity, motive, or a possible benefit from the outcome.

Concentrated authority under CCP rule makes regulatory accountability especially important. A government claiming extensive powers of organization and social control should explain how it uses them to prevent serious cross-border harm. When presented with specific warnings, it should account for action and inaction. National security cannot be emphasized only when obedience is demanded, then become a reason to refuse questions about regulatory decisions.

Corporate crime, regulatory failure, protection of favored interests, and state direction involve different actors and responsibilities. AMARVEL and Berry provide records of particular criminal responsibility; the cases do not themselves establish that the CCP's highest leadership directed the entire fentanyl network. An investigation moving upward must add connections, allowing its conclusions to advance with the chain it uncovers.

The liberal judgment remains clear: using public power to protect transactions that harm others, suppress investigation, or prevent victims from seeking accountability deserves exposure and constraint. People are entitled to demand records that permit independent examination of government. Officials cannot permanently turn what others cannot see into a domain they never have to explain.

## Access to power can become an asset

From criminal networks, we turn to a different kind of international relationship: status, personal connections, and commercial opportunity. Nuomt's written recaps of Lude commentary circulating in September 2026 connect British aristocratic standing, concerns about royal continuity, and interests involving China. One central question they raise is whether inherited status can be converted into opportunities for exchange, making its holders dependent on outside support.[5](93-notes.md#ch09-i05)

That question can be pursued through particular relationships. An intermediary who opens otherwise inaccessible doors offers more than professional services. Access to a prominent person may attract investors; bringing opportunities and support may, in turn, deepen that person's trust in the intermediary. Mutual need can give repeated contact increasing significance.

Britain's H6 case left a record of such a relationship. A December 2024 open judgment cited letters from an adviser to the Duke of York. One, dated March 2020, described H6's access and the trust placed in him. Another, from October, authorized him to represent the Duke's Eurasia Fund initiative in discussions with prospective partners and investors in China.[6](93-notes.md#ch09-i06)

The tribunal discussed the pressures on the Duke, his appreciation of loyal support, and the resulting vulnerability to misuse of influence. The national-security assessment also concerned H6's concealed links to the CCP and its United Front Work Department. Acknowledging limited evidence and a finely balanced assessment, the tribunal nevertheless upheld his exclusion. This was review of a national-security exclusion decision, not a criminal espionage conviction.[6](93-notes.md#ch09-i06)

The case gives informal influence a concrete structure to examine. Influence need not begin with a public instruction. It may begin with trust, changing who receives access, whose proposals are heard, and who can retain a position difficult for others to enter.

This book opposes inherited standing becoming a special entrance that requires no explanation. When the prestige attached to status affects public affairs, interests should be disclosed and independently examined. British authorities' restrictive action in H6 also shows that relationships involving royal figures can conflict with state institutions' judgments. Such conflict creates an opening for scrutiny.

## How recognition can shape today's choices

Benefits extend beyond money. Someone who already holds office or possesses wealth may still value a title after retirement, social recognition, or admission to a particular circle. If an award matters greatly and obtaining it depends on a few people's support, anticipation alone may influence present conduct. An advance contract of exchange is not necessary for that incentive to exist.

A 2004 British parliamentary inquiry into honours recorded historian Peter Hennessy's account of honours being used to soften dismissed ministers' discontent during the 1962 reshuffle. It also presented David Cannadine's historical interpretation of imperial honours as a shared hierarchy connecting British and local elites. In 2012, the relevant committee criticized arrangements for retiring ministers' awards and the Prime Minister's bypassing of the committee concerned.[7](93-notes.md#ch09-i07)

These accounts make the relationship between recognition and power more specific. Those able to recommend, approve, or block an award may carry weight within a relationship. Understanding how elites consider their future requires locating the actual points of decision, which helps explain whose wishes they have reasons to accommodate.

The monarchy also retains channels for personally conferring certain honours. Tony Blair's appointment to the Order of the Garter, announced at the end of 2021 and effective on New Year's Day 2022, was explicitly the Queen's personal gift, without prime-ministerial advice.[8](93-notes.md#ch09-i08) A channel of recognition controlled by the monarch therefore exists. Whether it influenced someone's earlier policy toward China requires following that individual's expectations and choices.

From a liberal perspective, the process by which future rewards enter public decisions needs constraint. An officeholder who sacrifices the public's interests for a personal honour betrays the responsibilities of office even without accepting cash. Public recommendation procedures, explanations of conflicts of interest, and opportunities to challenge decisions can reduce the room for exchanges concealed within ceremony.

## When financial pressure becomes dependence

The Lude recaps also propose that the financial demands of maintaining royal position and lifestyle may give outside funders influence.[5](93-notes.md#ch09-i05) The relevant mechanism turns on how much the recipient needs continuing support and whether affordable alternatives exist. A provider able to withdraw resources may gain bargaining power in the next disagreement if the recipient cannot readily replace them.

Assessing dependence means identifying the accounts to which money belongs. Britain's Sovereign Grant supports official duties and associated palace expenditure. It amounted to £31 million in the 2012–13 financial year and £86.3 million in 2021–22, with the latter including Buckingham Palace reservicing funds. These two nominal amounts do not support a claim that this government grant declined every year. Nor do they measure personal disposable living expenses.[9](93-notes.md#ch09-i09)

An investigation of outside funding should therefore identify the particular recipient: an individual, company, fund, or charity. Was money actually paid? Could the recipient control it? What did the provider expect in return? A company valuation, a proposed budget, and money already received can create different degrees of dependence; they cannot substitute for one another in the analysis.

An institution may receive rising public funding while individuals still pursue private commercial interests. A transaction originating in China also requires an inquiry into control and conditions before it can be attributed to a CCP institution. Financial records provide a point of reference. Influence turns on support that can be continued or withdrawn, and on the choices a recipient makes to retain it.

H6 provides material about access, authorization, and influence risk. This chapter has not established a payment chain through which the CCP filled a shortfall in the Queen's personal finances, or connected royal relationships and the drug cases as one organization. What can be compared here is how dependence shapes conduct, and how the public can acquire the ability to examine that process.

## Different interests within a trade exchange

Trade policy brings these questions to a more public negotiating table. On 1 October 2024, Canada imposed a 100% surtax on covered electric vehicles made in China, on top of the existing 6.1% most-favoured-nation tariff. The United States also raised its relevant Section 301 tariff on Chinese EVs to 100%, effective from 27 September that year. Their tariff policies then moved in a similar direction.[10](93-notes.md#ch09-i10)

Canada changed its arrangement in 2026. From 1 March, it repealed the 100% surtax and allowed an initial annual quota of 49,000 Chinese EVs to enter at the 6.1% most-favoured-nation rate. Its government announced corresponding Chinese measures that month, including a lower combined applied tariff on Canadian canola seed and suspension of anti-discrimination tariffs on canola meal, peas, and specified seafood until the end of 2026.[11](93-notes.md#ch09-i11)

Different interests become visible within this exchange. Restored market access can directly benefit producers affected by export restrictions. Import access can create opportunities for vehicle importers and consumers, while exposing competing producers and workers to additional pressure. Sharing a passport does not automatically give these groups identical policy preferences.

Beijing can use access to the Chinese market, and restrictions on it, as bargaining instruments. Foreign governments must weigh export gains, industrial consequences, and other risks. Industries heavily dependent on a market may place greater value on preserving the relationship and press their own government toward accommodation. Trade arrangements can thus create a path of influence into domestic politics.

This policy change already offers a concrete exchange to analyze without first presuming a royal instruction. Lobbying, undisclosed interests, or private intervention should enter the explanation if further investigation establishes them. The available trade records first reveal who may gain what and what the government exchanged.

Alliance membership does not erase these disagreements. Governments can cooperate on some security matters while facing different industry pressures and electoral demands. Evaluating a compromise requires disclosure of benefits and costs, examination of risk assessments, and participation by those bearing losses. Calling an agreement the national interest does not yet explain how its benefits are distributed.

## When cooperation starts to loosen

The more valuable a relationship becomes, the more disagreement its participants may tolerate to preserve it. The harder it becomes to leave, the more power may accrue to whoever controls an essential resource. Benefits and exit costs jointly shape cooperation; a handshake at a particular moment reveals only part of the relationship.

The trade arrangement offers a place to observe change. Canada's implementing notice of August 2026 continued quota access for the second six-month period, within a quota year beginning on 1 March.[11](93-notes.md#ch09-i11) Continuing cooperation still faces changing interests as an arrangement operates. Alternative markets, political pressure from industry losses, or higher security risks may change the price of continuing cooperation.

Personal relationships can involve similar calculations. An intermediary who supplies scarce opportunities may gain trust. When the legal or reputational costs of association outweigh the benefits, the recipient may want greater distance. To preserve the relationship, the other party may offer more or apply pressure. The outcome depends on the options each still possesses.

Leaving also has conditions. Within criminal organizations, the risk of prosecution and a shared history of participation may increase exit costs. In lawful transactions, long-term investments, contractual commitments, and a lack of substitutes can also make departure expensive. These mechanisms of dependence can be compared while the legal character of each activity and individual responsibility remain matters of particular conduct.

Applied to international politics, common interests can sustain cooperation without eliminating separate objectives. One compromise does not guarantee a permanent alliance; one disagreement need not end a relationship. To assess whether a change is substantial, follow the movement of resources, the execution of commitments, and whether participants bear real costs in changing their relationships.

## Bringing costs back to those who decide

The cases and arrangements in this chapter approach a common problem from different directions. When gains accrue to participants and costs fall on people excluded from the decision, who can force the former to reconsider? Fentanyl crime, royal access, honours, and trade negotiations each require appropriate investigation and constraints. All show why accountability cannot stop at a grand collective name.

Criminal investigations must connect participation, knowledge, and money within cases that can be heard. Officeholders and their associates should face independent scrutiny of contacts, conflicts of interest, and interventions. Governments should disclose the terms of trade exchanges and the basis of risk judgments, allowing affected people to contest decisions and seek change. Scrutiny must enter where power actually operates.

International relationships particularly need that capacity. Businesses can seek opportunities across borders, and political figures can seek support through different channels. Individuals bearing the consequences may have only one institution immediately available to petition. If every participant points elsewhere, a division of labor can make responsibility disappear. Independent investigation and cross-border law enforcement can reconnect separated stages to the harm they helped produce.

A critique of CCP rule and its external relationships should reveal where the public is excluded: who acquires the authority to decide for the country, who can exchange public resources without explanation, and who can prevent victims from asking questions. Liberalism requires placing those powers within the reach of the people affected, so they can examine, limit, and correct their exercise.

That capacity has a prerequisite: people must have an opportunity to learn what happened. Who keeps records, who may investigate, and who decides whether information is published affect every preceding link. The next chapter examines control over information, and how independent reporting, research, and disclosure help ordinary people constrain power.
