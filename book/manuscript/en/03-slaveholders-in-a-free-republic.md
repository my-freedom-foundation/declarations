# Chapter Three · Slaveholders in a Free Republic

*In the People's Name: Privilege, Revolution, and Unaccountable Power · English draft v0.3 · 17 September 2026*

## First, account for the unpaid work

A letter dated 7 August 1865, signed Jourdon Anderson in Dayton, Ohio, presented a former enslaver in Tennessee with an account. At the rates set out in the letter, Anderson and his wife Mandy's past work amounted to $11,680, with interest to be added and clothing and specified medical expenses deducted. The recipient had written asking him to return.[1](93-notes.md#ch03-a01)

Dictated by Anderson and included in Lydia Maria Child's 1865 collection, The Freedmen's Book, the letter turned the former enslaver's invitation into a claim for payment. The recipient wanted to recover a source of labour. Anderson demanded recognition that this labour belonged to people entitled to be paid for it.

The account changed the terms of the conversation. The former enslaver could express a wish; Anderson could set terms and examine the promise. Work once treated as an entitlement was being reckoned as time deserving payment. Those years now appeared as more than a record of the enslaver's production. They became a worker's claim upon the past.

The previous chapter ended with promises a free republic had yet to fulfil. This chapter follows a particular privilege: holding other people as property and having public institutions help sustain that relationship. How did it obtain protection within a republic, and how was it abolished? After abolition, could someone safely determine the course of their own life?

Anderson's demand belongs to the transition between these conditions. To understand why a reply could carry such weight, we first need to return to the country's institutional foundations.

## How a republic accommodated slavery

The Constitution of 1787 prohibited the United States and individual states from granting titles of nobility while accommodating slavery. Its original Article I counted three-fifths of the enslaved population toward apportioning House seats and direct taxes. Article IV required the return, upon claim, of people escaping from legally imposed service or labour in one state into another.[2](93-notes.md#ch03-a02)

These arrangements expose a division within republican government. Rejecting noble titles did not automatically exclude ownership of human beings. A country could oppose one form of hereditary public authority while giving legal protection to another form of inherited domination.

The three-fifths provision turned population into a political resource. Compared with excluding the enslaved population from the count altogether, this apportionment formula increased slaveholding states' weight in the distribution of House seats, while the people counted had no vote. Workers supplied labour under an enslaver's control and also entered a calculation that increased slaveholding states' representation.

These powers could reinforce each other: law protected ownership, while representation could be used to preserve that law. Equality in representation depends on whether those represented can influence their representatives. When others exercise the political weight contributed by a person, a gap remains between counting a population and obtaining its members' authorization.

The fugitive service clause likewise separated crossing a state boundary from escaping domination. Leaving a place did not, by itself, remove a person from the obligation of return. Enforcing an enslaver's control thus extended beyond a plantation and beyond the arrangements of a single state. The privilege required cooperation elsewhere and recognition within a shared political order.

These rules supported enslavers' control beyond their own private force. As long as people could be returned across state lines, departure offered no reliable escape from control over their labour. As long as common institutions recognized that control, ending it required changes in representation and the state's obligations. Slavery's survival thus involved the organization of public power.

## Whose freedom to leave?

On 24 December 1860, South Carolina adopted its declaration explaining secession from the Union. The document invoked the principles of changing government and independence asserted in 1776. It also charged non-slaveholding states with obstructing the fugitive service provision and denying property rights in enslaved people.[3](93-notes.md#ch03-a03)

Two demands to leave now pointed in opposite directions. Secessionists claimed the state's entitlement to withdraw from a common political arrangement while demanding that this arrangement help recover people who had left enslavement. A state's autonomy and a person's autonomy received different treatment within the same argument.

Local self-government can constrain distant authority or protect local rulers. The distinction lies in the rights of local residents. If the new decision-makers can still own people, leaving the Union may preserve enslavers' control while closing off enslaved people's own route of exit.

Texas's declaration, adopted on 2 February 1861, made the boundary especially explicit. It defended slavery, reserved equal civil and political rights for white men, and justified the continued subordination of Black people.[4](93-notes.md#ch03-a04)

Free government and racial domination were not two concepts later historians had to force together. The secessionists' own document placed them together. The equality it defended had a boundary of status. Within it, people demanded protection against interference; beyond it, others were made to sustain their lives and power.

These declarations establish what secessionists publicly defended. They do not establish every charge against the North or describe the motives of every Southern resident or soldier. Texas also offered other reasons. Yet slavery occupied an explicit and important place in these formal arguments. Reducing the dispute to abstract states' rights removes the specific relationship those rights were being invoked to protect.

The privilege examined in this book therefore has a clear object: some people demanded that the state uphold their domination of others. When the latter's freedom was treated as an injury to the former's interests, the dispute exceeded an ordinary policy disagreement. Whether people could act as persons in their own right was itself at stake.

## Connecting the Union's survival to emancipation

Slavery's place in wartime policy involved different forces connecting their objectives to the survival of a state. Secessionists sought protection for enslavers' rights. The Union had to decide how to address slavery within its official objective of saving the Union. Each side's purposes and their development need to be understood through its own statements and actions.

In his public reply to Horace Greeley of 22 August 1862, Lincoln described saving the Union as his foremost official objective. He explained his choices about slavery by whether they would advance that objective, while retaining at the letter's end his personal desire for universal freedom.[5](93-notes.md#ch03-a05)

Personal aspiration and presidential duty supplied two connected kinds of reason. The wish for universal freedom expressed Lincoln's public statement of values. Explaining policy through the Union's survival placed action within war aims and presidential authority. How the two came together depended on the legal form given to emancipation and who would enforce it.

The National Archives records that Lincoln had already read an emancipation draft to his Cabinet in July that year and issued the preliminary proclamation on 22 September.[6](93-notes.md#ch03-a06) His August explanation of presidential duty thus belonged to the same process as preparations for emancipation already underway.

The final Emancipation Proclamation of 1 January 1863 invoked the President's wartime authority as commander-in-chief. It declared freedom for people enslaved in designated rebellious states and parts of states and committed federal executive and military authorities to maintaining that freedom. It also announced acceptance of suitable people into armed service.[7](93-notes.md#ch03-a07)

It did not abolish all slavery throughout the country that day. Loyal slaveholding border states fell outside its scope, and the text listed exempted areas. Making freedom effective depended on federal military advances and protection. These limits help explain both how the measure operated and what it changed: the Union publicly incorporated emancipation into the war and committed state power to supporting it.

State power could be used to pursue people escaping slavery or to protect their freedom. The crucial change concerned the rules authorizing it and whose protection it owed. A relationship of domination once sustained by common institutions was beginning to face their opposition.

Enslaved people and Black soldiers were not merely recipients of presidential decisions. Their actions and demands also changed the questions the country had to answer.

## Bearing the danger, receiving less pay

On 28 September 1863, Corporal James Henry Gooding of the 54th Massachusetts Infantry wrote to Lincoln from Morris Island, South Carolina. He reported a paymaster's offer of ten dollars a month. Another three dollars might follow congressional action, but there was no guarantee.[8](93-notes.md#ch03-a08)

Gooding grounded the dispute in the duties the soldiers had already performed. Having fulfilled a soldier's obligations, why should they not receive equal pay? He also asked the United States to apply to its own payroll the equal treatment it demanded from the enemy for its soldiers.

His argument had a force similar to the freedom petition in the previous chapter. Those making the demand invoked a principle already recognized by its recipient and turned the test inward. A standard the country asserted externally became an obligation it had to explain to its own people.

Three dollars might seem small in an account book while mattering considerably to a household. Gooding specifically connected it to fuel for wives and children.[8](93-notes.md#ch03-a08) Equality here concerned both standing and livelihood. Calculating a soldier's pay also expressed how the country valued his service.

Demanding fair treatment did not make the letter a rejection of the Union's cause. Gooding petitioned as a participant, asking the country he supported to correct an injustice. That relationship matters: loyalty need not require surrendering the right to complain. Criticism can emerge from taking a shared principle seriously.

Gooding's appeal connected the service the country needed to the treatment its soldiers demanded. Sharing the danger brought unequal pay under more direct challenge, and people seeking freedom advanced standards of their own. The country representing them still had to answer for how it treated them.

## Putting abolition into the Constitution

Ending slavery nationally required a legal foundation extending beyond a wartime measure. The Thirteenth Amendment passed Congress on 31 January 1865 and secured the necessary state ratifications on 6 December. It prohibited slavery and involuntary servitude within the United States and places under its jurisdiction, retained an exception for punishment following due conviction, and authorized congressional enforcement.[9](93-notes.md#ch03-a09)

The two stages of congressional passage and state ratification gave the change a legal foundation different from a wartime order. The presidential order applied to designated war zones; the constitutional rule bound institutions across the country. Freedom's legal basis expanded from wartime authority into an enduring national obligation.

Ending ownership of persons was a fundamental change. The previously protected relationship lost its legal foundation. Someone's assertion that they or their ancestors had owned another person no longer sufficed to require the state to preserve that status. Freedom acquired an institutional basis independent of the former enslaver's permission.

The change first removed a class of demands a former enslaver could lawfully make. Differences in property, education and bargaining strength would still shape life afterward. Even someone entitled to their own labour could find it difficult to reject terms when they urgently needed income and lacked alternative work. Securing personal freedom left the question of how other rules would affect that choice.

The punishment exception also required constraints on the state's power to convict. If a group was especially vulnerable to punishment because of the circumstances in which its members earned a living, their labour could again become subject to coercive arrangements. Each measure's legality still requires separate judgment. The question here is how powers over crime, punishment and the use of labour could affect freedom already acquired.

The question to pursue is how labour, residence and punishment would be arranged once the old means of control lost their foundation.

## New restrictions on freedom

Also in 1865, Mississippi enacted laws directed at Black people. Under their provisions, from the second Monday of January 1866, specified groups over eighteen without lawful employment or business could face punishment as vagrants. Failure to pay specified fines within five days could lead a sheriff to hire out their labour to cover fines and costs.[10](93-notes.md#ch03-a10)

These rules connected the circumstances of making a living to punishment. Someone without work meeting the law's requirements could face a fine. Inability to pay could place their labour at an official's disposal for hiring to someone else. The choices necessary for free labour would narrow through that sequence of procedures.

When prescribing written labour contracts lasting more than a month for the specified Black workers, the same excerpts required forfeiture of that year's wages up to departure if a worker left before the agreed term expired without good cause.[10](93-notes.md#ch03-a10) This raised the cost of leaving. The longer a person had already worked, the more wages departure could cost, giving the worker greater reason to endure conditions they wished to reject.

These statutory excerpts reveal pressures created by law. How those pressures operated locally, how many people were affected and whether appeals succeeded still require enforcement records. Even before estimating those outcomes, the connections among fines, hiring out labour and forfeiting wages show how law could narrow the choices involved in free labour.

The mechanism itself raises a serious question. A system can acknowledge free people in its language while restricting their actions through employment qualifications, penalties and the allocation of labour. To assess whether freedom has taken root, we must examine what happens when people lose work, reject terms or want to leave.

That examination allows us to recognize abolition's substance while continuing to investigate what remained unresolved. Prohibiting ownership of people established a new basis for challenging the old order. Turning that basis into a stable life still required constraints on those controlling punishment and resources.

## From freedom from ownership to equal citizenship

The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, recognized citizenship for people born or naturalized in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction. It required states to observe due process when depriving people of life, liberty or property and to provide equal protection to everyone within their jurisdiction. The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, prohibited federal or state denial or restriction of citizens' voting rights because of race, colour or prior servitude.[11](93-notes.md#ch03-a11)

These amendments advanced different questions. Once free from ownership, who would be recognized as a member of the political community? What protection could people subject to state authority require? Could they influence those exercising power through voting?

These three protections addressed connected questions of power. Abolition removed an enslaver's ownership of a person. Citizenship and equal protection established grounds for demanding the state's protection. Voting safeguards concerned people's ability to influence those making public decisions. Each earlier change opened space to pursue the next, while subsequent safeguards could help sustain freedoms already gained.

The Fifteenth Amendment prohibited voting discrimination on specified grounds; it did not remove every qualification through a single provision. Delivering the legal promise still required institutions, enforcement and remedies. Its value lay in establishing a standard by which people could hold the state responsible. Whether that standard was honoured required continuing scrutiny.

Enslavers, their opponents, federal and state authorities, and people seeking freedom made different demands throughout this process. National institutions were both instruments of domination and objects of struggle for change. Abolition's achievement belonged to a historical transformation that deprived a previously protected form of domination of its legitimacy.

Recognizing that achievement strengthens the principle of freedom. It shows that arrangements permitted at a country's founding need not remain unchangeable. The demands of those injured by them can become grounds for reassessing the shared legal order.

## Who can refuse?

Return to Anderson's letter of 1865. He said he had obtained freedom papers in 1864 and asked about his daughters' safety and schooling for Black children.[1](93-notes.md#ch03-a01) Those concerns brought a vast institutional change back to a family's future.

The former enslaver's promise of better treatment no longer sufficed to decide whether Anderson would return. A person with purposes of his own could examine that promise. Payment for work, protection for children and confidence in the future were questions in which he was entitled to participate.

This is the chapter's concrete answer to the problem of privilege. Overcoming privileged rule requires removing particular powers to dominate others while giving those formerly dominated the conditions for exercising freedom. Evidence that power has been constrained lies in relationships where they can make demands, refuse arrangements and obtain protection.

American abolition leaves the book with two connected insights. Principles of freedom can help people change profound injustices within their own country's institutions. After a fundamental victory, they must still examine whether control persists through other rules. The second task sustains the first achievement.

The next chapter, In the Name of Equality, turns to communism's account of class and oppression. We will first understand what it promises to resolve, then ask how workers can constrain an organization claiming to represent them and assuming power to remake society.

Anderson's account gives that question a tangible starting point. A person's labour, family and future should be matters in which they participate as someone entitled to set conditions. Every promise of liberation must answer who will ultimately hold that power of decision.
