# Chapter Eight · Hong Kong After the Handover

*In the People's Name: Privilege, Revolution, and Unaccountable Power · English draft v0.2 · 15 September 2026*

## Sovereignty changes hands: how do residents gain a say?

The previous chapter traced how families and organizations influence the allocation of supreme power. Hong Kong brings the question into the relationship between residents and rulers. Who may change the conditions of life, who must bear the consequences, and who can make a decision-maker reconsider? The handover provides a historical turning point through which to examine those relationships.

In her Christmas broadcast of 1997, Elizabeth II looked back on Hong Kong's handover. The Prince of Wales had represented Britain at the occasion, she recalled, placing the event within Britain's farewell to its old empire. For the monarchy, a historical chapter had closed.[1](93-notes.md#ch08-hk01)

For Hong Kong's residents, life continued. There was still work to do, bills to pay and family to care for. People still needed to know who could make decisions affecting their lives, and whom they could challenge when they were treated unfairly. A state can declare a historical mission accomplished. A ceremony cannot, by itself, secure an individual's rights.

Hong Kong therefore offers an important place to examine power. Three questions belong together: Who holds sovereignty? Who makes the rules? Who can make the rule-makers answer for their decisions? These questions are related, but a handover does not automatically give them the same answer.

Examining what followed also requires an accurate account of colonial government. The legislature's historical record identifies 1991 as the year of the first direct elections to the Legislative Council. During the long period of British rule, this channel for residents to choose legislators emerged very late.[2](93-notes.md#ch08-hk02) Colonial government itself left a problem of inadequate authorization by residents.

Ending colonial rule can have profound significance. Whether residents acquire stronger control over their government remains a separate question. The standard for measuring rights should not change with the nationality of the ruler. A person can value Chinese sovereignty while insisting that those who govern honour their commitments to Hong Kong's residents. Institutions must answer both concerns.

## How promises become usable rights

A political settlement can meet different participants’ needs. Governments seek sovereignty and an orderly system of government, investors value continuity in capital and contracts, and residents need both stable lives and protection for expressing their views. These interests can support one another. When they conflict, the capacity to negotiate, withdraw or obtain a remedy helps determine which demands receive priority.

The 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration provided for China to resume the exercise of sovereignty over Hong Kong on 1 July 1997. It set out protections concerning autonomy, the judiciary and residents' rights, continued free-port and financial-centre arrangements and capital flows, and required appropriate consideration of British and other foreign economic interests. The specified basic policies were to remain unchanged for fifty years.[3](93-notes.md#ch08-hk03)

State interests, investor expectations and residents’ rights thus entered the same settlement. Economic relationships could survive political change, and preserving that continuity could become a shared interest supporting cooperation. How long cooperation lasts, and which disagreements change it, depend on how participants calculate their respective benefits and costs.

Commercial continuity can matter greatly to ordinary people. Employment, savings and long-term contracts depend on reasonably stable rules. The difficult question arises when the stability sought by investors conflicts with demands for political participation. Who judges that conflict? Who can object? Who has the means to secure a correction? A document may promise to protect several interests without giving their holders equally powerful means of defending them.

Article 27 of the Basic Law lists freedoms including speech, the press, publication, association and assembly. Article 31 addresses movement and emigration, while Article 35 provides for access to courts and judicial remedies. These are legal commitments with identifiable content.[4](93-notes.md#ch08-hk04) They supply standards against which actual policies can be assessed, beyond the reassurance their wording may offer.

Rights most need protection when those in power consider a statement dangerous, an organization troublesome or an activity contrary to national interests. If rulers can both define a threat and decide whether opponents may challenge them, the boundaries of rights can shift with governing needs. Independent scrutiny of restrictions, opportunities to challenge decisions and timely remedies make commitments useful to unpopular people as well.

Hong Kong's experience also asks us to be more specific about the rule of law. It matters whether rules are public. It matters, too, who can change them, what constrains those who interpret them, and whether an affected person has a usable route of appeal. Legal texts begin the inquiry. Their operation determines their weight in people's lives.

## A bank across the handover

HSBC’s history shows an institution adjusting its position across a political transition. In evidence submitted to the British Parliament, it explained that, following its purchase of the remaining shares in Midland Bank in 1992, regulatory requirements led it to move its group headquarters to London from 1 January 1993.[5](93-notes.md#ch08-hk05)

The group headquarters in London could coexist with continuing operations in Hong Kong. On 13 January 2026, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority continued to identify the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation, Standard Chartered Bank (Hong Kong), and Bank of China (Hong Kong) as the territory's three note-issuing banks.[6](93-notes.md#ch08-hk06) A multinational group's organization and a bank's local role can coexist.

Institutions and residents can possess very different resources for responding to changes in the rules. Where legal and commercial conditions permit, a large institution can adjust the location of its headquarters, capital and operations while maintaining relationships with different governments. If one party can move business and the other values that activity, both have an incentive to keep negotiating. Mutual need can sustain cooperation while leaving room for pressure on each side.

There is a further step between an advantage in resources and privilege. Does that advantage secure exceptions others cannot obtain, transfer losses to people excluded from decisions, or prevent responsibility from being enforced? Specific transactions and regulatory decisions can test these mechanisms. The account of institutional continuity and bargaining here does not establish royal involvement in a concealed transaction.

Here, the position of residents is worth comparing with that of large institutions. Even when an individual can leave a city, they cannot take its account rules, retirement system and dispute-resolution bodies with them. Using property that belongs to them on paper still depends on particular institutions. Disputes over retirement savings bring that distinction into focus.

## Leaving the city, accessing the savings

The Mandatory Provident Fund, or MPF, is Hong Kong's compulsory retirement savings system. Its regulator's published rules allow early withdrawal in specified circumstances, including permanent departure from Hong Kong. An applicant must make the relevant declaration and provide evidence satisfying the trustee that they have permission to reside outside Hong Kong.[9](93-notes.md#ch08-hk09)

On 10 March 2021, the Mandatory Provident Fund Schemes Authority, or MPFA, stated that a British National (Overseas) passport—a BN(O) passport—or an associated visa could not be relied on to support an early-withdrawal application on permanent-departure grounds. The statement also required trustees to examine all the evidence and facts of an application.[7](93-notes.md#ch08-hk07)

The dispute first concerns eligibility to access savings early on permanent-departure grounds. In a response issued in 2023, the MPFA said that funds remained in members’ individual accounts after applications were rejected, emphasizing individual assessment.[8](93-notes.md#ch08-hk08)

Control over the acceptance of evidence also controls the point at which eligibility takes effect. Savings may remain in a person’s name, but a rejected application prevents them being used through that application to establish a new life. The migrant bears the costs of moving while explaining their residence status to institutions left behind. Obtaining reasons, supplying further evidence and challenging decisions directly affect their ability to use their property.

A voluntary survey published by Hong Kong Watch in August 2026 collected accounts of such difficulties. Of 153 respondents, 52 said they had applied after obtaining settled status in Britain. They reported forty rejected applications, nine pending and three successful. These voluntary responses cannot be used to estimate outcomes for all applicants.[10](93-notes.md#ch08-hk10)

Reports of rejection after obtaining settled status bring the scrutiny of evidence into focus. If new material still fails to meet the requirements, institutions should explain what is missing and how equivalent circumstances receive consistent treatment. Otherwise, even an applicant willing to follow the procedure may be unable to work out what would secure recognition of their eligibility.

The general rules require evidence concerning permanent departure and residence abroad.[9](93-notes.md#ch08-hk09) Establishing whether these refusals arose from insufficient material, interpretation of the rules or inconsistent administration still requires the applications and reasons for refusal. Comparing those explanations would locate responsibility in decisions that can be corrected.

A retirement system can restrict early access to protect income in later life. That purpose remains subject to standards of fairness, transparency and proportionality. If evidentiary demands grow while reasons for refusal remain unclear, the burden of complying falls chiefly on applicants. Published standards, clear reasons and effective review constrain the conversion of this imbalance into arbitrary control.

The dispute brings a chain of responsibility into view: a policy passes through a regulator and financial institutions to become a decision temporarily preventing someone from using their retirement savings. For the person affected, the practical question is whether there is an institution required to give reasons, capable of correcting mistakes and willing to take responsibility. If responsibility is repeatedly passed between institutions, pursuing a complaint can become more than an individual can reasonably manage.

## How a newspaper loses the means to publish

A newspaper needs journalists, editors, printing and payment systems to work together. If a crucial part loses access to funds, reporting may no longer reach readers. Control over the means of payment can therefore alter the space for expression, with effects that can precede a final determination of the matter under investigation.

Hong Kong's National Security Law took effect on 30 June 2020. Article 4 expressly refers to freedoms including the press and publication; Article 43 provides investigative measures including freezing relevant assets and requiring the removal of information. The implementing rules permit the Secretary for Security to issue a freezing notice on reasonable suspicion, while allowing affected parties to apply to the Court of First Instance to revoke a notice or obtain a licence.[11](93-notes.md#ch08-hk11)

The force of a judicial remedy depends on whether it can keep pace with the consequences of a measure. If a media organization loses the ability to pay staff, retain its team or publish before effective review occurs, a later remedy may not restore the interrupted reporting. The scope of freezing powers, licences for ordinary operating needs and the timing of scrutiny are therefore substantive questions of press freedom.

In a September 2021 statement, Next Digital's board described the asset restrictions imposed that June. The board said they prevented the company from lawfully paying journalists, buying ink and paying electricity bills, leading it to close its Hong Kong operations. Apple Daily ceased publication on 24 June. [12](93-notes.md#ch08-hk12)

The Hong Kong government's public explanation that year emphasized that the freezing measures addressed suspected collusion with foreign or external forces. It invoked Article 4 in maintaining that freedoms including the press remained protected.[13](93-notes.md#ch08-hk13) This defence grounds the measures’ legitimacy in national security and criminal investigation.

The government’s allegations and the consequences described by the board must face each other within the same examination of rights. Given the potential to prevent an entire newspaper from operating, the government should explain why that scope is necessary, how the effects connect to the investigation’s purpose and how affected people can challenge the decision in time. Invoking a press-freedom provision again cannot answer for those consequences.

Such powers can also change others’ expectations. If publishers and journalists believe continued operation depends on avoiding the displeasure of authorities controlling access to funds, they may reduce sensitive investigations before receiving a direct order. This is an inference about incentives that requires further examination through specific conduct. It shows why measures against one institution can affect wider public discussion.

In its 2022 concluding observations on Hong Kong, the UN Human Rights Committee expressed concern about the interpretation and application of the National Security Law. It identified media closures, searches, asset freezes and arrests of journalists among its concerns about expression, recommending protection of editorial independence and an end to the use of relevant laws against legitimate expression. [14](93-notes.md#ch08-hk14)

For residents, the importance of press freedom also extends beyond supporting a particular newspaper. Someone can dislike a publication's politics while insisting that measures removing its means of operation require adequate justification and effective scrutiny. Protecting dissent is often hardest when the person or institution needing protection is unpopular with those in power or with a majority.

This book therefore argues that timely, independent and effective review is necessary to protect rights when investigative measures can stop a media organization from operating. National-security grounds require a specific connection to the measures and an explanation of their scope. Affected parties must be able to challenge restrictions before the harm becomes difficult to reverse.

## Whom can the ballot constrain?

Disputes over expression and property eventually reach the political structure. If residents consider a policy unreasonable, how can they change the people who make it? If their objections are repeatedly rejected, can elections provide an effective means of correction?

Articles 45 and 68 of the Basic Law address the selection of the Chief Executive and the Legislative Council, identifying universal suffrage as the ultimate aim; the Chief Executive provision also specifies a nomination procedure. Article 158 places the power to interpret the Basic Law with the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress while providing for interpretation by Hong Kong courts when adjudicating cases.[4](93-notes.md#ch08-hk04) These provisions locate local political and judicial powers within a central institutional framework.

The changes of 2021 supply figures that can be compared. The government's yearbook records that the previous Legislative Council had seventy seats, thirty-five directly elected through geographical constituencies. The election held on 19 December 2021 under the new arrangements was for a ninety-seat legislature: forty members returned by the Election Committee, thirty by functional constituencies and twenty by direct geographical elections.[15](93-notes.md#ch08-hk15)

Directly elected geographical seats fell from thirty-five to twenty while the legislature grew, reducing the seats residents directly determined through geographical constituencies. This book regards the contraction of that channel of direct public choice as a retreat in political accountability. Different seats have their own selection procedures, and changing who selects officeholders changes whose demands they need to answer.

If residents struggle to change policy through their votes, public objections are less likely to threaten an officeholder’s continued power. Individual complaints may receive attention while the rules producing similar disputes persist. The allocation of executive, legislative, judicial and central powers therefore also determines whether correcting a case can lead to changing a policy.

In assessing Hong Kong’s governance under CCP leadership, this book insists that residents must have practical power to constrain their rulers. A government demanding obedience to law should subject its own authority to enforceable limits. A government promising freedom should permit affected people to challenge measures taken in the name of security. Its willingness to accept that challenge is where the promise is tested.

## Returning the question to residents

Hong Kong places history, finance and personal rights on the same timeline. The monarchy's account of departure, the two governments' commitments, a multinational bank's organization, the documents a regulator accepts, a newspaper's ability to pay its bills, and the seats residents can directly elect each reveal a different location of power.

These relationships can involve common interests and conflicts. Large institutions have incentives to preserve business, regulators control recognition and administration, political leaders care about the direction of government, and residents must bear the consequences in daily life. If the costs fall chiefly on people with little part in deciding, institutions may continue to operate while the practical use of rights contracts.

For this book, Hong Kong's central question is whether people incorporated into a grand historical settlement also acquire the power to defend their own rights. Commercial activity, international recognition and legal promises matter. Whether people can safely object, obtain reasons, challenge decisions and make those in power face consequences determines how such arrangements actually treat them.

The next chapter follows how interests encourage cooperation across borders and how responsibility becomes dispersed within it. Hong Kong leaves several leads: shared benefits can sustain relationships, the costs of leaving can change bargaining power, and divided responsibilities can make accountability harder to obtain. Understanding these mechanisms requires keeping sight of who bears the final cost.
