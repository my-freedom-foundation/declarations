# Chapter Four · In the Name of Equality

*Privilege and Freedom: Who Makes the Rules, Who Pays the Price · English draft v0.3 · 17 September 2026*

## Who Decides About the Workers' Dining Room?

In her 1921 pamphlet on the Workers' Opposition, Alexandra Kollontai described workers in Soviet Russia trying to organize dining rooms, nurseries, and the transport of firewood. Repeated referrals, delays, and refusals wore down their initiative. She traced the problem to a separation: working people stood on one side, officials with the power to decide on the other.[1](93-notes.md#ch04-eq01)

Following this party critic's account, the problem lies in the separation of need from authority. People needing a dining room bear the cost of waiting; those able to approve it hold the power to advance or shelve the project. If workers struggle to hold anyone responsible for delays, officials may give greater weight to approval requirements than to workers' needs.

In the previous chapter, Anderson set conditions for paid work when addressing his former enslaver. Gooding demanded equal military pay from a country he supported. This chapter turns to a body of thought promising a more comprehensive transformation of society. It argued that legal freedom alone could not overcome economic domination: the relations of production had to change, and class rule had to end.

This promise brings resources and political authority into the same question. After the old rulers are removed, the institutions controlling production can still shape people's lives. If workers depend on those institutions both for income and for the opportunity to dissent, liberation needs a more concrete safeguard: those represented must retain rights to constrain their representatives.

We will return to Kollontai's problem. First, we need to understand why this movement connected the reconstruction of society so closely to political power.

## Explaining Oppression Through Production

In the 1848 *Manifesto of the Communist Party*, Marx and Engels explained history through class conflict and located the central antagonism of modern capitalism between the bourgeoisie and the proletariat. They credited the bourgeoisie with breaking apart older relationships, developing productive forces, and creating a world market. They also criticized wage workers' dependence on capital and the discipline of the factory.[2](93-notes.md#ch04-eq02)

Within this analysis, class concerns a person's position in the relations of production. Who owns the means of production? Who must sell their capacity to work in order to live? Who determines the conditions of that work? These questions reach beyond whether an individual happens to be generous. Wealth and poverty are visible conditions, but the explanation cannot stop at separating kind people from unkind ones.

This approach shifts attention from an isolated injustice to arrangements capable of producing injustice repeatedly. An employer may pay what a contract requires while workers lack meaningful alternatives. The law may permit everyone to enter contracts while the consequences of losing an income leave the parties with very different bargaining power.

Economic dependence differs fundamentally from ownership of a person. Abolition, examined in the previous chapter, removed an enslaver's legal ownership and established essential grounds for workers to demand payment, leave, and choose again. The constraints on choices in wage labor depend on labor markets, property, law, and the ability to organize. Improving those conditions matters because people who have gained personal freedom deserve more reliable means of directing their lives.

This book takes one question from that analysis: how do resources become authority? When rejecting working conditions may cost someone their main income, while the other party can more readily find a replacement, their capacities to withstand refusal differ. Examining this relationship requires comparing actual alternatives and capacities to organize, rather than predicting every conflict's outcome from a person's class alone.

The *Manifesto*'s recognition of the bourgeoisie's development of productive forces clarifies the issue. Marx and Engels sought to overcome the class relationships they believed accompanied those developments. Recognizing the value of expanding production and examining workers' control over its conditions can belong to the same analysis.

## Into Whose Hands Does Capital Pass?

Part II of the *Manifesto* distinguishes abolishing bourgeois property from depriving individuals of the appropriation needed to sustain life. It also calls for the proletariat to win political power, progressively take capital from the bourgeoisie, and concentrate the instruments of production in the state. In that passage, the state is understood as the proletariat organized as the ruling class.[3](93-notes.md#ch04-eq03)

The proposed change reaches far beyond adding a benefit or adjusting a tax. It concerns ownership of the conditions of production and authority over how production proceeds. Among the listed measures is the abolition of inheritance. The conclusion of Part IV explicitly retains a commitment to overthrow existing social conditions by force. At the same time, Part II presents the ending of class rule and the free development of individuals as its horizon.[3](93-notes.md#ch04-eq03)

This remedy seeks to change a minority's control over a majority's labor through productive property, while concentrating extensive economic authority in a new public power. Conditions of production previously controlled by owners would be administered by institutions responsible for public production. Whether that authority is constrained depends on the relationship workers establish with those institutions.

A class is a social group; a state exercises power through particular institutions and people. Connecting the two requires arrangements for selection, scrutiny, removal, and appeal. If workers can inspect accounts, question allocation, and replace officials, public resources have a basis for being used under rules workers help determine. If officials control those opportunities, representatives may gradually become people on whom others must depend.

Concentration carries a particular risk: the institution arranging production may also control workers' access to income and opportunities to express an opinion. If critics fear consequences for their work and resources, they may first consider how to retain officials' trust. Even officials initially sincere about serving workers would then have fewer opportunities to hear unwelcome views.

A transfer of ownership should therefore also change the distribution of authority and powers to hold decision-makers responsible. Work, access to resources, and examination of accounts all require procedures workers can use. Those allocating resources must remain bound by the procedures especially when workers refuse an arrangement.

## Equality Has More Than One Meaning

In the *Critique of the Gotha Programme*, written in 1875, Marx distinguished different stages of communist society. In the phase emerging from capitalism, he envisaged individual receipts linked to labor contributed, after deductions for common needs. Distribution according to need would become possible only in a higher phase, after further changes in productive capacity and social relationships.[4](93-notes.md#ch04-eq04)

Equality here does not simply mean giving everyone an identical share. The same standard of labor can apply to people with different physical capacities, family responsibilities, and needs. A uniform measure does not guarantee identical circumstances.

Meeting different needs means someone must judge which needs take priority and how available resources should be distributed. Where resources are limited and circumstances differ, the allocator's information and discretion shape the result. Public rules, individual choice, and appeals against mistakes are therefore also part of equality: people needing assistance should be able to explain their circumstances and challenge adverse decisions.

Part IV of the same work identifies a political transition between capitalism and communism, during which the state would take the form of the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat.[4](93-notes.md#ch04-eq04) The ultimate goal of liberation and the coercive power of the transition thus belong to the same conception.

The transition creates a particular authority: deciding when the conditions for ending coercion have been met. If those exercising coercion always make that judgment, retaining power may conflict with declaring the transition complete. When present restrictions can always be explained as necessary for future freedom, people living through the transition struggle to demand delivery of the promise.

This book therefore distinguishes a goal from the authority claimed in its pursuit. A social goal may extend far into the future, while the present powers of those who govern still require limits. The hope of ending oppression does not relieve decision-makers of the obligation to give reasons, or deprive affected people of standing to examine their decisions.

## Whom Should the State Obey?

Marx did recognize the danger of a state standing above society. In *The Civil War in France* in 1871, he praised the Paris Commune's arrangements for election, recall, and public service paid at workers' wages. In their 1872 preface to the *Manifesto*, Marx and Engels acknowledged that some specific measures had become outdated. Drawing on the Commune, they emphasized that workers could not simply take over the existing state machinery and use it for their own purposes.[5](93-notes.md#ch04-eq05)

The arrangements they praised sought to change the relationship between public officials and society. Election makes holding office depend on support; recall allows that support to be withdrawn; wage limits reduce the scope for obtaining high pay through office. The subject here is their political proposal. How the Commune actually implemented these arrangements remains a separate historical question.

The *Critique of the Gotha Programme* connected freedom more directly to making the state subordinate to society, rather than freeing the state from constraint.[4](93-notes.md#ch04-eq04) Under that standard, later rulers claiming Marx's inheritance should also accept workers' choice of officials, scrutiny of resources, and preservation of their own organizations. Subordination to society should take the form of rights through which its members can make the state change decisions.

Limited pay, recall, and elections suggest practical ways to constrain power. Each needs procedures that can be used. A provision for recall still leaves us asking who may initiate it, who can put forward an alternative candidate, and whether organizing opposition might itself bring punishment.

Power can also yield benefits through channels other than pay. A person deciding access to housing, jobs, and information may make others depend on their permission even while receiving a modest salary. Wage limits need to work alongside constraints on allocation and accountability for abuse, making public office harder to turn into a resource for personal domination.

People within organizations must ultimately put these safeguards into effect. Who can become an official, and how members express trust or opposition, affect whether written arrangements can be used. The next step therefore turns to the revolutionary organization itself: how does it select leaders, and how does it treat those seeking a change in direction?

## From Spontaneous Action to Professional Revolutionaries

In *What Is To Be Done?*, published in 1902, Lenin distinguished the trade-union consciousness produced by spontaneous workers' struggles from a comprehensive socialist political consciousness. He argued that the latter had to be introduced from outside spontaneous economic struggle. He also explicitly recognized that workers could contribute to socialist thought as theorists.[6](93-notes.md#ch04-eq06)

Lenin's account of trade-union consciousness included demands for labor legislation. His distinction lay between immediate economic struggle and a broader socialist revolutionary direction. Authority to determine that direction would affect who qualified to lead: an organizer's authority rested both on the ability to coordinate action and on a claimed grasp of comprehensive political knowledge.

He also distinguished broad workers' organizations from a smaller, secret organization of professional revolutionaries, and called for workers to be trained for the latter. Under repression by the tsarist police, he rejected applying full publicity and general elections directly to a clandestine organization. He emphasized rigorous selection, training, the trust of comrades, and internal responsibility instead.[6](93-notes.md#ch04-eq06)

In underground activity, secrecy and rigorous selection had a rationale in preventing police infiltration. At the same time, entry required existing members' approval, giving the leadership control over admission. If this structure continued after the acquisition of state power, arrangements for protecting a secret organization could exclude the wider public from selection and scrutiny. Moving from an underground organization to a governing institution requires changing precisely this relationship of authorization.

Taking risks and possessing experience may explain why people trust an organizer. They do not automatically confer a permanent right to govern. Trust among members of an underground organization cannot substitute for authorization from everyone subject to state power.

If the organization believes it better understands workers' fundamental interests, dissent may be interpreted as workers' failure to grasp the overall direction. The less its judgment is accepted, the more the organization may believe stronger leadership is needed. Breaking that cycle requires people outside that leadership's control to be able to present evidence, join together to express their views, and seek new elections. Otherwise, the organization's own interests can readily be expressed as the common interests of workers.

Training workers to become leaders can change the leadership's social origins. Preserving workers' ability to constrain those leaders changes the relationship of representation. The two can support each other, but neither replaces the other.

## Before the State Withers Away

In *The State and Revolution*, written in 1917 and first published in 1918, Lenin again proposed measures against bureaucracy: elected officials, recall at any time, and remuneration at ordinary workers' wages. He retained representative institutions and the elective principle while calling for a transformation of parliament into a body doing the actual work of legislation and execution.[7](93-notes.md#ch04-eq07)

The same work described the transition as an expansion of democracy for the laboring majority alongside the forcible suppression of exploiters and restrictions on their freedom. The complete disappearance of the state depended on the disappearance of classes and further economic and social conditions. The text supplied no definite timetable or concrete form for that outcome.[7](93-notes.md#ch04-eq07)

The proposal places popular participation and class coercion within the same transition. A new state power first eliminates the conditions of old domination, then moves toward a society without a separate coercive apparatus. Determining who belongs to the participating majority and who is subject to suppression therefore becomes a crucial power over the boundaries of rights.

The difficulty arises while that destination remains unreached. Who decides whether someone belongs among the exploiters to be suppressed? How can a mistaken classification be appealed? Can opposition to a policy be treated as opposition to the whole project of liberation? Can people entitled to recall ordinary officials also challenge the leadership that determines these classifications?

Class conflict and security threats may give reasons for action; particular accusations still require independent examination. If classification alone deprives accused people of standing to respond, the leadership can use classification to avoid answering opposition. The ability to present evidence and obtain a remedy is necessary to prevent the same procedures from protecting both error and the rulers' own position.

A promise that the state will eventually wither away offers no present remedy to someone subject to its power. A transition without a known endpoint has particular need of clear responsibilities now. Otherwise, the language of ultimately abolishing domination can justify expanding it in the present without accountability.

This institutional danger also became a dispute within the revolution: could the organizers' own power block the experience and correction that social transformation needed? Critics supporting socialism raised related warnings from this point.

## Warnings From Within the Revolution

In *The Russian Revolution*, written in 1918, Rosa Luxemburg criticized freedom reserved for the government's supporters or for a single party. Without open competition among views, elections, and freedom of the press and assembly, she argued, public institutions would lose their vitality and bureaucracy would monopolize initiative. The work was first published in 1922.[8](93-notes.md#ch04-eq08)

Luxemburg wrote as a supporter of socialist revolution. She recognized the pressures of war and isolation while opposing the elevation of measures adopted under those pressures into a general theoretical model. Learning from popular experience and open correction was a possibility she wanted revolutionary transformation to preserve.[8](93-notes.md#ch04-eq08)

This argument connects freedom to knowledge. No program can anticipate all the needs of every factory, household, and locality. Allowing affected people to disagree is one way to discover problems. Treating disagreement only as an obstruction risks shutting out the information needed to correct mistakes.

Kollontai's examples of dining rooms and nurseries brought the issue into everyday administration. She also criticized appointments that detached officials from accountability below, and called for elections and wider discussion within the party. She continued to support Communist Party leadership and advocated purging non-proletarian elements from the party. Her proposed reforms thus remained within the framework of party leadership, combining support for initiative from below with limits on political participation.[1](93-notes.md#ch04-eq01)

These disagreements show that people claiming to represent workers can dispute what workers' interests actually are. Sharing an ideal does not make their judgments identical, much less make one side permanently correct.

To change decisions, internal critics need to keep speaking, join with others who share their views, and retain opportunities to advance alternatives. If each person can only raise a problem individually with superiors, the leadership can handle each separately while critics struggle to form a common source of constraint. The right to join together to express dissent therefore became central to the next dispute.

## How Unity Changes Criticism

On March 16, 1921, the Tenth Congress of the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks) adopted its resolution *On Party Unity*. Among its reasons for emphasizing unity was the danger that hostile forces could exploit disagreement. It required the immediate dissolution of groups formed around their own platforms and made refusal grounds for expulsion from the party.[9](93-notes.md#ch04-eq09)

The resolution retained requirements for criticism and correction, but placed criticism within discussions involving party members generally and prohibited separate groups organized around platforms. It also required serious consideration of practical proposals against bureaucracy and for greater workers' initiative. Central authorities received disciplinary powers over factional activity, with a special meeting and voting threshold for extreme measures against Central Committee members.[9](93-notes.md#ch04-eq09)

What changed was the way an opinion could become collective action. An individual's ability to raise a problem through prescribed channels differs from the ability of several people to coordinate around a shared platform over time. The latter can help people keep an issue alive, support one another, and develop alternatives. Prohibiting it affects whether criticism can accumulate into a force capable of changing the leadership's direction.

Organizations do need discipline and may face deliberate disruption. But when leaders can decide which forms of coordination constitute a faction and impose penalties accordingly, those seeking to scrutinize them confront organizational powers held by the people being scrutinized. A considerable distance can open between permission to speak and permission to mount an effective challenge.

Hostile forces exploiting disagreement was the risk asserted by the resolution; whether particular members engaged in disruption still requires judgment of their actions. The formal change is clear: criticism remained recognized, while groups organized around independent platforms faced dissolution and discipline. Under such rules, even without a particular punishment being imposed, members might reduce coordination for fear of being classified as a faction. The extent of actual discipline and its effects still requires enforcement records.

The opening contradiction now becomes clearer. An organization claiming to rest on popular initiative can encourage participation while restricting the collective forms through which people influence its leadership. If leaders alone determine the scope of participation, the people's status as the source of power lacks reliable protection.

## Liberation Must Include Limits on the Liberators

Connecting the chapter's questions reveals a possible path toward concentrated power: productive resources pass to public institutions, a revolutionary organization leads those institutions, and the organization limits workers' ability to form independent opposition. This path concerns proposals to end class rule through transformed ownership and revolutionary power. It cannot simply be applied to every public service, social benefit, or improvement in working conditions.

These texts also retain demands for election, recall, the state's subordination to society, and workers' initiative. The conflict between these directions must be assessed where rights are exercised. Do elections admit alternatives? Can recall reach the real decision-makers? Can opposition cost people the basis of their organization and livelihood?

An institution can declare that assets belong to everyone while preventing the public from examining their allocation. A party can claim to represent workers while denying them the ability to organize an alternative. A leader can promise the eventual disappearance of classes while deciding today who qualifies to dissent. Where these relationships exist, the names assigned to institutions cannot demonstrate that people hold power.

This book rejects the conversion of a supposedly correct ideal into an entitlement to represent others without constraint. A theory must face examination against evidence. A policy must remain open to public correction. A relationship of representation must permit a new choice. People escaping oppression should not lose the ability to refuse in the name of their own liberation.

The dining room in Kollontai's account is more than a question of administrative efficiency. It brings a vast promise back to an ordinary human scale. Can people help arrange their own lives? Can they make those acting on their behalf answer for their decisions? The same measure applies to a state claiming to build an entirely new society for its people.

The next chapter, *After the Revolution, Who Owns the State?*, turns to the Chinese Communist Party's organization and governing practices through appointments, policies, and the experiences of people affected by them. This chapter's questions will guide the inquiry: how do resources, appointments, and discipline interact, and what rights can affected people use to change decisions? China's particular facts and responsibilities must be established through those practices.

Ending an old privilege cannot excuse new power from accountability. A person's labor, dignity, and life do not cease to belong to that person because a leader claims to know the direction of history.
