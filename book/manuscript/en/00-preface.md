# Preface　From a Declaration to a Book

*English draft v0.2 · 17 September 2026*

## Freedom cannot belong only to the victors

In 2022, I wrote a declaration about freedom in mainland China. Beginning with the equal rights of every person, it opposed the Chinese Communist Party's arbitrary control of public power and called for people to be able to choose a government that genuinely protects their rights. The declaration made allegations and expressed a clear political position: no party can own a country, and no leader can permanently decide on the people's behalf how they must live.

This book develops that position into an inquiry into power. A declaration can express refusal. Readers also need to understand how the relations of domination being rejected arise, why they endure, and how they can reappear within revolutions that seem to oppose privilege. People need ways to constrain new power after rejecting the old. Otherwise, freedom may remain a slogan at the moment of victory without reaching ordinary lives.

This book takes a liberal position. Individuals are not expendable material for an organization's ideals or resources that any historical mission may dispose of at will. Government must answer to the governed for its legitimacy. An assessment of policy must include those who bear its consequences. Political victory cannot remove the duty to give reasons, accept scrutiny, and relinquish power. These requirements apply to the CCP and to everyone who opposes it and promises to bring freedom.

I want readers to understand the dangers within communist revolution and CCP rule, particularly the relations of domination that arise when an organization claims to know history's direction, monopolizes representation of the people, and can suppress scrutiny of itself. Explaining this danger requires more than repeating adjectives. We must see who allocates resources, who determines a person's status and prospects, who can punish dissent, and where an injured person can still turn for help.

## Start with concrete relationships in history

The book begins with inherited status. Before someone has made any choice, law and custom may already have determined their position. It then turns to American independence and abolition, examining how principles of freedom were asserted and how they conflicted with continuing domination. Removing one privilege does not remove every other privilege. Writing down a right does not mean that everyone can already exercise it.

Communist theory, revolutionary organization, and the CCP's party-state power form the next focus. Ideals, organizational rules, and actual conduct need to be distinguished. The promise of equality can mobilize people; control over resources and appointments can determine anew who must obey whom. We will ask how an organization representing workers can be constrained by those workers, and who determines the direction of the state after a revolution.

Reform, 1989, succession at the highest level, and Hong Kong bring these questions into specific choices. When economic activity expands, does political power also become revocable? When a public promise is eroded, can residents require its fulfillment? When countries and institutions cooperate for advantage, can responsibility lose its bearer amid the division of tasks across borders?

The final chapters turn to information, pandemic warnings, and public responsibility. How can someone with knowledge but little reputation bring material to institutions capable of investigating? How should professional judgment confront financial, career, and organizational interests? Technology can help preserve evidence, track responses, and publish corrections, but it can also control new points of access. The book returns, finally, to whether ordinary people can ask questions safely and make the powerful answer.

The chapters select historical material and cases directly relevant to these questions. They do not compress all human history into two camps that always act as one, or treat countries, families, and institutions as internally undivided. Interests can bring people together and drive them apart. Understanding power requires following specific decisions rather than stopping at the name of an identity or relationship.

## Make judgments and explain their basis

Faced with power skilled at concealment, waiting for it to volunteer every relevant record often means leaving judgment in its hands. Public political language cannot simply be treated as a statement of actual motives either. This book therefore uses inference, examining how opportunities to know, interests, relationships, and conduct can make an explanation stronger. Historical and political judgment need not always await a final court decision.

Inference carries responsibilities of its own. It should identify its premises, distinguish independent material from repetitions of one account, and explain what discoveries would require revision. Sources cannot be counted as equal votes. One person with genuine knowledge may carry more weight than many institutions repeating a common account. Respecting a minority voice also requires showing readers where that weight comes from.

I do not ask readers to accept every conclusion before they are entitled to read this book. What matters is that they can see how conclusions were reached and find a way to challenge them and investigate further. Chapter Eleven distinguishes its argument about pandemic responsibility from the key elements that remain unproven. Protection for people making disclosures, preservation of records, correction of retaliation, and investigation of failures can begin while broader inquiries continue.

The original declaration faces the same scrutiny. The appendix explains its claims individually, what the available material supports, and what requires correction or further investigation. Preserving a historical text does not require later research to submit to every sentence it contains. A commitment to freedom should withstand revisions of fact and protect the people who identify errors.

## Bring judgment back to life

I hope readers leave this book with questions they can continue to use. Who made this decision? Who benefits, and who bears the risk? Can those who disagree speak safely? Who keeps the records? Who has authority to examine them independently, and what corrections can they require? When these questions repeatedly go unanswered, grand claims to represent the people deserve closer examination.

Asking them does not require everyone to make the same political choices, or everyone living under oppression to bear the same risks. Freedom includes the ability to refuse domination and the room to decide how to live in one's own circumstances. We can value courageous people while working to reduce the sacrifices required to tell the truth.

This book begins with a declaration against arbitrary power and seeks to make freedom a requirement binding everyone who holds power. Only when people who disagree with us can retain their dignity, ask questions, and change their choices does freedom move beyond a declaration and into our shared life.
