# Chapter Seven · Family, Organization and Supreme Power

*Privilege and Freedom: Who Makes the Rules, Who Pays the Price · English draft v0.4 · 17 September 2026*

## The political assets a family leaves behind

Obtaining supreme power involves more than the allocation of an office. It also involves who will trust a candidate, support him, and place their own futures in his hands. An appointment announcement records the outcome. Understanding how that outcome develops requires examining relationships, trust, and shared interests.

In a birthday letter to his father, Xi Zhongxun, dated October 15, 2001, Xi Jinping identified communist conviction as a quality he hoped to inherit. He emphasized that his father had continued to believe in the Communist Party while suffering false accusations and adversity.[1](93-notes.md#ch07-f01) In this son's tribute, family suffering did not end political allegiance. It became evidence of enduring faith.

This offers a way to understand political continuity. Someone who has suffered persecution may blame particular struggles or individuals while retaining his identification with the ruling organization and its common enterprise. Returning to the center of power can then mean restoring what he regards as his family's proper standing.

Families also leave more than beliefs. In *Forever a Son of the Yellow Earth*, Xi recalled reading newspaper denunciations of his father while living in northern Shaanxi. He also described how his father's earlier activities in the area encouraged people to protect and help him.[2](93-notes.md#ch07-f02) His account illustrates a distinction between losing office and losing every relationship.

Reputation, old acquaintances, and trust can outlast an appointment. Although they may never appear in an asset register, they can influence whose difficulties receive attention, who obtains an introduction, and who retains an opportunity to return after a political setback. Understanding family advantage requires following these opportunities across generations.

The liberal judgment of this book is clear: individuals are not the property of a revolutionary organization, and the state is not the estate of distinguished families. Family memory can explain someone's desire to govern. Authority over other people still requires authorization from those who will be governed.

## The “1938 line”: seniority and the inner circle

Lude's account of a “1938 line” provides a specific framework for examining informal power. Nuomt's November 2021 program summaries describe those who joined the Communist Party before 1938 and their descendants as a core group. They allege that arrangements made after 1989 reserved succession for this group and recount a claimed promise by Jiang Zemin to Deng Xiaoping to return power.[3](93-notes.md#ch07-f03) The following analysis uses this framework to explore seniority, trust, and inheritance. The particular secret threshold and handover promise remain claims attributed to the source.

The year has an identifiable organizational context. Party histories record a March 15, 1938 resolution calling for large-scale recruitment, linking expansion to the enlargement and consolidation of the anti-Japanese national united front.[4](93-notes.md#ch07-f14) Early members of an expanding organization may share memories and ways of recognizing one another that distinguish them from later recruits.

The analogy with a company's founding team helps explain the mechanism of trust. People who have shared prolonged risks may know one another's histories, vulnerabilities, and commitments. A capable newcomer may still need an introduction from an established member to gain access to closer relationships. If assessments depend on such familiarity, seniority becomes more than a date on a résumé. It can become the authority to vouch for someone else.

Family relationships offer a means of extending that credit. An elder who knows a younger person's childhood and family, and remains close to their relatives, may regard that person as more predictable. The younger generation can inherit opportunities for introductions, serious consideration, and participation in the selection of others. Membership may expand without a comparable expansion of the group that makes the final choices.

Public biographies record Xi Zhongxun joining the Youth League in 1926 and the Party in 1928, Deng joining the Party in 1924, and Bo Yibo in 1925.[5](93-notes.md#ch07-f04) These families fall within the early period under discussion. The boundaries of an inner circle, however, also depend on rank, alliances, and recognized family ties. Jiang Zehui's recollection records her father, Jiang Shangqing, joining the Party in 1929 and Jiang Zemin being adopted as his son after his death.[6](93-notes.md#ch07-f05) Joining dates or kinship labels alone cannot fully describe who counts as an insider.

Two explanations would lead to different conclusions. A relatively closed group of early revolutionary families might constrain the choice of supreme leader. Alternatively, family background might confer substantial advantages while effective support shifts with working relationships, offices, and alliances. The value of examining the “1938 line” is that it brings the groups behind individual candidates into view. Establishing a uniform succession threshold requires asking whether this seniority is indispensable and who can veto whom.

Whether the outcome is a closed threshold or overlapping networks, a country cannot be treated as the founding team's private company. Shared struggle may create mutual trust. It cannot bind everyone outside the circle, or their descendants, to permanent obedience.

## How struggle changes people's choices

Ruthless struggle can change the conduct of those who remain, as well as eliminate competitors. Suppose political suspicion can quickly lead to detention. Defending a suspect may then put the defender at risk. Silence, conformity, and withholding information acquire a protective value. If extreme declarations also demonstrate loyalty, members face pressure to compete in displays of hardness.

This is an argument about incentives. Someone initially willing to express compassion may withdraw an objection under threat. Those who refuse to participate may leave, be marginalized, or become targets themselves. Those willing to use cruel methods may appear more dependable. When protection rests on a superior's confidence, it becomes harder to refuse an order by invoking a rule that applies to everyone.

A publicly available transcription of the Soviet Areas Central Bureau's January 7, 1932 resolution on the suppression of counterrevolution provides a historical point of reference. The document acknowledges expanding suspicion, reliance on coerced confessions, arbitrary killing, and uncontrolled powers within the suppression apparatus. The Central Bureau also accepts responsibility for failures of leadership. Yet the resolution continues to defend the campaign against the AB League and the suppression at Futian.[7](93-notes.md#ch07-f14) The organization acknowledged severe harm while continuing to define the limits of correction through the survival of its revolutionary camp.

A liberal account must condemn coercive interrogation and arbitrary killing. Preserving revolutionary strength cannot justify them. When the organization conducting a struggle retains the authority to decide who may be harmed, victims have little prospect of obtaining a remedy independent of the victors.

Violent selection can turn ruthlessness into a competitive advantage. Restraint becomes sustainable only when people can refuse unlawful orders, defend the accused, and present adverse evidence without inviting retaliation. Otherwise, the pressure to survive can overwhelm personal goodwill.

This mechanism explains more than a judgment of everyone's character based on birth or the year they survived to. Survival alone cannot distinguish perpetrators from victims or people forced to comply. Responsibility attaches to conduct. The institutional question is what makes cruelty profitable and compassion or dissent costly.

## From personal access to political recommendation

Relationships can first influence whether a request is heard. Someone with access to a crucial decision-maker may introduce another person, explain a mistake, or obtain an opportunity for consideration. When a small group of acquaintances controls entry to decision-making, access itself becomes a political resource.

In a 2002 interview, Nie Weiping recalled playing bridge with Deng on numerous occasions from the 1980s onward. He said Deng's secretary prohibited personal requests during the games, but also recalled raising the separation of a married athletic couple and an issue concerning bridge competitions. A report published in 2013 quoted his recollections of discussing Go with Xi Jinping.[8](93-notes.md#ch07-f16) These personal accounts make concrete how informal contact can provide a channel for requests.

A Nuomt post published on May 1, 2022, in a thread identifying an April 29 Lude program, goes further. It alleges that Nie helped Xi obtain work as Geng Biao's secretary and later spoke to Deng on Xi's behalf, securing Deng's approval.[9](93-notes.md#ch07-f16) This recommendation claim has a traceable source; its specific sequence and effect on supreme succession have not been independently established.

Following that explanation, a recommendation does more than transmit praise. An intermediary can lend credibility to a candidate, making his background seem understandable and his conduct predictable. The beneficiary may attribute an opportunity to that relationship and feel an incentive to preserve or repay it. If appointments develop mainly through these channels, the public procedure may reveal a choice already formed.

Private acquaintance is not inherently an abuse of power, and recommendations can convey useful information. Privilege arises when insiders obtain substantive consideration through relationships while outsiders lack comparably effective and publicly assessable routes into competition. Candidates' abilities can be evaluated separately. Controlling who gets evaluated is itself a form of power.

## Losing power as the erosion of a support network

A circle can carry shared interests without its members always remaining united. Scarce offices can go to only some of them. Others must consider who will protect their futures. Common origins may make mutual recognition easier while bringing competition closer to the center.

In examining competition over succession at the highest level, we can consider Bo Xilai as a possible contender. The key question is how that competition unfolds, beyond looking backward for signs of the final result. As Bo Yibo's son, he had an early revolutionary family background. After losing his Chongqing post in 2012, he also faced suspension at the central level, investigation, and expulsion decisions. In 2013, a court sentenced him at first instance to life imprisonment for bribery, embezzlement, and abuse of power.[10](93-notes.md#ch07-f06)

A local post provides direct organizational resources. Losing it is a blow, but personal contacts, central supporters' assessments, and expectations of a return need not disappear on the same day. If people able to provide protection or influence appointments remain willing to help, dismissal from one local office need not end political competition. This is a mechanism for analyzing a fall, rather than a claim about which people Bo still controlled.

Further sanctions can change those expectations. When a person moves from being a potential returnee to someone whose supporters face danger, allies may reconsider. Fear can loosen connections without a public order to sever them. Following an elite struggle therefore requires examining both the offices removed and the people who lose the capacity to act on someone else's behalf.

The Ling Jihua affair can be approached in this way. Bo's removal in Chongqing was announced on March 15, 2012; the crash involving Ling Jihua’s son, Ling Gu, occurred on March 18. Reporting published in December 2012, drawing on anonymous Party insiders, linked the subsequent cover-up and its exposure to diminished influence for Hu Jintao and Ling Jihua in succession negotiations.[11](93-notes.md#ch07-f15) This chronology places the crash within a struggle already under way. It does not make dismissal from local office the endpoint of the entire contest.

If a leader relies on a principal aide for communication, coordination, and personnel channels, a blow to that aide can weaken a wider support network. Interpreting Ling's fall as a setback for Hu's side therefore has a plausible political mechanism. Whether the crash was deliberately caused is a separate question. The available material does not establish a chain of responsibility showing Xi ordered it.

Liberalism also cannot rely on an elite understanding that relatives will be spared. Everyone's relatives should be protected against political retaliation, with individual responsibility assessed separately. Rights that survive a person's fall offer more dependable protection than membership in a favored circle.

## Offices turn relationships into decision-making authority

Informal relationships can shape the selection of people. Formal office allows some of their choices to take organizational effect. Appointing personnel, allocating resources, and disciplining cadres generally require institutions able to issue effective decisions. Studying influence behind the scenes therefore includes following it into these procedures.

The 2012 and 2022 Party constitutions give the Central Committee a five-year term. Its plenary session elects the general secretary from among the Politburo Standing Committee, while the Central Committee decides the membership of the Party's Central Military Commission. The relevant provisions impose no two-consecutive-term ceiling on the general secretary or Party military commission chair. They also retain provisions against lifelong tenure and personality cults and in favor of collective leadership.[12](93-notes.md#ch07-f07)

On November 15, 2012, the first plenum of the Eighteenth Central Committee elected Xi general secretary and decided that he would chair the Party military commission. The corresponding lists published on October 23, 2022 again named him in both positions.[13](93-notes.md#ch07-f08) These outcomes locate the concentration of organizational authority.

An analysis of relationships asks how candidates emerged before formal selection, what support was exchanged, and what opposition would cost. Relationships can bring someone into office; office can then allow him to influence later appointments. Their interaction can generate a widening advantage that extends far beyond an elder's initial recommendation.

Two developments must be distinguished. A leader may continue to carry out a supporting group's common arrangements. Or, after gaining support, he may gradually weaken those who initially constrained him. The former preserves the group's control over its representative; the latter moves toward personal domination. To distinguish them, examine who retains an effective veto, rather than assuming each purge follows a plan written in advance.

Both arrangements leave citizens with the same problem of authorization: ordinary people do not vote as citizens to choose the Party's general secretary. One insider's ability to veto another does not give outsiders the right to reject the governing arrangement as a whole.

## How exit rules change competition

Political competition determines both who enters office and who must leave. Predictable exit rules affect everyone's calculations. Potential successors can anticipate opportunities, supporters can prepare to change relationships, and incumbents know that their positions have a predetermined boundary.

The constitutional amendment adopted on March 11, 2018 removed the two-consecutive-term limit for the state president and vice president. It retained the provision tying each term to that of the National People's Congress.[14](93-notes.md#ch07-f09) Xi was elected state president in 2013, 2018, and 2023, beginning a third consecutive term in 2023.[15](93-notes.md#ch07-f10)

The general secretary had never been subject to the presidency's two-term ceiling, so the amendment must be understood in relation to the specific office. Its political significance remains: an explicit boundary that could have applied when its time came was removed from the rules of a state position.

This book regards the deletion of that existing constraint as an institutional retreat. It cleared an obstacle to continued re-election without giving citizens greater powers to choose and replace their rulers. That criticism does not depend on establishing a personal decision to remain in office for life.

The incentive is straightforward. The less certain the exit date, the more others may stake their futures on continuing to enjoy the incumbent's confidence. If raising succession itself is treated as disloyal, open discussion of a replacement becomes harder. Under those conditions, procedures can take place on schedule while substantive choice narrows around the same individual.

A constraint on power must remain effective when the person holding office wants to stay. Otherwise, departure depends chiefly on personal willingness or another internal struggle, with the public still outside the arrangement.

## How power converges on one center

Control over appointments and control over decisions can reinforce each other. Promoted officials may become more dependent on the person who advanced them. Concentrating more decisions in the same center increases the value of its confidence. This process can progressively strengthen personal authority.

The 2017 Nineteenth Party Congress report called for upholding the Party center's authority and centralized, unified leadership, describing the Party as the highest political leadership across fields.[16](93-notes.md#ch07-f11) In 2018, the constitutional amendment added the statement that Party leadership is the defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics to Article 1. Party leadership had already appeared in the preamble.[17](93-notes.md#ch07-f09)

The 2018 institutional reform plan converted central leading groups for deepening reform, financial and economic affairs, foreign affairs, and cybersecurity and informatization into commissions, seeking stronger central Party coordination. Reporting on the first meeting of the Central Commission for Comprehensively Deepening Reform identified Xi as its director.[18](93-notes.md#ch07-f12) Institutional reorganization and a specific appointment together illustrate the organizational direction of centralization.

Compared with the 1987 proposal for separating Party and government functions discussed in the previous chapter, the emphasis moved from distinguishing functions toward stronger coordination by the Party center. Both approaches retained Party leadership. The issue for this book is how the change affects room for disagreement: where a decision can be reconsidered, who can require objections to remain on the record, and who can halt a mistaken policy's execution.

Central coordination may accelerate policy implementation. It also concentrates the pressure involved in correcting mistakes. If different bodies must demonstrate loyalty to the same center, separate responsibilities may not provide effective checks. When something goes wrong, officials may first seek to explain it as their own failure to carry out instructions, rather than question the decision itself.

This danger does not require the supreme leader to decide every matter personally. If subordinates believe certain preferences cannot be challenged, they may anticipate wishes, filter information, and interpret uncertain intentions more aggressively. The institutional criticism concerns how power induces obedience before an explicit order is given.

## Whose security sets the bottom line?

Political language states goals and shapes which conduct appears legitimate. The 2017 report defined the Party's original aspiration and mission in terms of the happiness of the Chinese people and national rejuvenation, while also affirming communist ideals and Party leadership.[19](93-notes.md#ch07-f11) It closely connected the organization, its historical task, and public welfare.

Following this logic, if the organization presents itself as indispensable to the historical task, opposition to its authority can be portrayed as a threat to the whole enterprise. The terms of disagreement shift. Citizens questioning who may decide can instead be required to prove that they are not endangering the collective future.

“Bottom-line thinking” should be examined within this political operation. An official 2014 reader explained it as anticipating adverse outcomes, preparing responses, and seeking better results. A report on a related 2019 seminar speech included requirements concerning political security, public-opinion guidance, internet governance, and young people's ideological and political education.[20](93-notes.md#ch07-f17) A public definition is no guarantee of actual intent. The analytical questions are whose losses count as unacceptable risks and who is restricted as a result.

If preserving the ruling position takes priority and individual rights lack independent protection, preventing risk can become a reason to expand control. The more officials depend on the top leadership, the more they may regard reducing criticism and suppressing unfavorable information as fulfilling their responsibilities. Under those conditions, the boundary between the public interest and the security of those in power can be blurred deliberately or otherwise.

Liberalism must preserve that distinction. The rights to criticize government peacefully, organize political opinion, and seek a change of leadership cannot be canceled to protect an incumbent's position. Security measures need defined limits and independent remedies for those restricted. An exalted mission cannot substitute for these constraints.

Analyzing political language this way can reveal the interests it may protect while leaving concealed motives open to investigation. Responsibility for particular orders must be connected to actions and participants. Institutional criticism and investigation of specific responsibility can proceed together; the former need not wait for every secret link to be uncovered.

## From internal protection to public accountability

Family advantages can continue through public office, access, information, and business relationships. When relatives' benefits are connected to a ruler's decisions, preserving political influence may also mean preserving economic interests. A fall can impose costs on an entire network as well as the individual.

An official explanation of 2022 rules governing business activities by officials' spouses, children, and children's spouses describes reporting to the organization, restrictions differentiated by rank and category, and responses to violations.[21](93-notes.md#ch07-f13) These arrangements identify conflicts between office and relatives' commercial interests as matters for regulation. Their operation depends on who can verify declarations, identify actual beneficiaries, and pursue concealment.

Within a network that relies on internal protection, knowledge about one another can serve two purposes. It may help enforce common rules or become a means of selective pressure. If enforcement depends chiefly on political relationships, a wrongdoer may escape consequences while protected and suddenly face punishment after that protection ends. This mechanism requires examination through specific cases; it is not a finding of corruption against every family.

The 2022 Party constitution places the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection under the leadership of the Central Committee and gives it responsibilities including supervision, discipline, accountability, and handling reports of misconduct.[22](93-notes.md#ch07-f07) The organization's ability to inspect and punish members is related to, but distinct from, the public's ability to hold the highest leadership accountable.

If the subject of an inquiry can influence investigators' appointments, the inquiry's scope, and disclosure of its materials, investigators may first calculate the political consequences. Effective accountability must overcome that obstacle. An investigation cannot stop because it reaches the highest level, nor can its conclusions lose force because a ruler objects.

The liberal safeguards must therefore be concrete. The public must be able to call for investigations, independent institutions to obtain evidence, victims to seek remedies, and governments to be peacefully replaced. The downfall of a prominent person does not automatically transfer these capacities to ordinary people.

## An insider's victory cannot replace the public's decision

This chapter has followed family relationships into trust and recommendation, organizational struggle into incentives for survival, and those incentives into offices, exit rules, and centralization. Its central inference is that when opportunity, security, and material interests repeatedly depend on a few people's protection, political participants may put maintaining relationships and winning struggles ahead of accepting constraints that apply to everyone.

Lude's “1938 line” interpretation directs attention to historical circles and succession arrangements behind individual résumés. Its specific threshold and secret promises remain to be tested. Research into informal power, however, need not depend on rulers' willingness to disclose every relationship. Competing explanations should be assessed by how they account for support, exclusion, and vetoes, without placing any source beyond question.

Whether supreme authority remains constrained by a group of families or becomes concentrated in someone gradually escaping his original patrons, liberalism asks the same question: can people outside the core circle decide who will govern them?

The state is not an estate for victors to distribute. Revolutionary seniority cannot confer authority over future generations of citizens. A political mission cannot cancel individual rights. However intense the competition among insiders, changing the winner cannot substitute for giving the public the power to decide.

What is needed is a system able to protect dissenters, stop arbitrary persecution, disclose adverse evidence, and bring rule to a peaceful end. It must work even when those in power resist its constraints. Freedom would then cease to depend on a family's favor, a faction's victory, or a leader's restraint.

The next chapter turns to Hong Kong. The interpretation and enforcement of promises made at the handover of sovereignty bring the question into sharper focus: can the public make those in power honor their commitments and obtain an effective remedy when those commitments are broken?
