# Chapter One · Born Unequal

*In the People's Name: Privilege, Revolution, and Unaccountable Power · English draft v0.3 · 17 September 2026*

## A status waiting before birth

In December 1662, Virginia's colonial legislature provided that children born there would be enslaved or free according to the condition of their mother.[1](93-notes.md#ch01-b01)

A short law attached a life, before its bearer had made any choice, to another person's existing status. The child's diligence, courage or willingness to obey was not the basis on which the rule determined freedom. The mother's position became a condition deciding the child's future.

The rule drew its line through the mother's condition: a free mother's child had free status, while an enslaved mother's child inherited enslavement. Bondage could thereby pass between generations. A child did not need to do wrong, incur a debt or agree to the arrangement to be placed under another person's control at birth.

This is where the book begins its inquiry into privilege. Privilege, as examined here, is an institutional advantage allowing some people to exercise power over others who struggle to refuse or demand accountability on equal terms. Birth can confer it, as can legal status or membership in an organization. Investigating it means identifying who can decide, who must live with the consequences, and how that relationship is sustained.

Differences in wealth, ability and material comfort can all affect people's choices. The question of privilege goes further, into a relationship backed by coercion. When the parties disagree, do the rules give one a superior position in advance? Does the other have a meaningful opportunity to demand equal treatment?

At birth, a person cannot choose their family or deliberate on the laws already waiting for them. If law turns ancestry into a basis for domination, part of a life is decided by others before any discussion of individual effort begins. Resistance to such an arrangement starts by demanding standing as an equal human being.

## Law can recognize freedom and organize enslavement

In the much earlier Institutes of Justinian, the account of personal legal status begins by distinguishing the free from the enslaved. It describes freedom as a natural capacity and slavery as an institution contrary to nature, subjecting someone to another's ownership. It nevertheless specifies legal ways of acquiring enslaved status.[2](93-notes.md#ch01-b02)

The juxtaposition deserves attention. The same legal language can acknowledge the importance of freedom while carefully regulating its deprivation. Recognizing a conflict with natural freedom does not necessarily lead those making the rules to abolish the relationship.

A legal system's coherence therefore cannot settle the question of justice. Its classifications may be clear, its procedures stable and its outcomes predictable, while it assigns people unequal legal positions. For someone placed in a subordinate category, stable rules can also mean that changing their situation is difficult.

The law also left routes for changing status. It discussed manumission, the granting of freedom to an enslaved person, and provided for a child to be free if the mother had been free at some point between conception and birth.[2](93-notes.md#ch01-b02) Such a rule could change a child's life. It also sharpened the question: why should freedom depend in the first place on when one's mother was free, or on an owner's willingness to release someone?

Yet a route for changing status and an equal right to freedom are different things. Assessing a system means examining who can obtain an exception and why anyone needs the exception in the first place. If someone must await a special decision to escape another person's ownership, they do not yet possess a standing that others enjoy without having to apply for it.

Rome and Virginia had different institutions. The question worth asking in both is who received which powers of decision under law. As long as one party could claim ownership of another, that relationship constrained the latter's opportunities to change their condition.

## How status outlives an individual

Return to Virginia. The rule of 1662 transmitted status to the next generation. Another act, from September 1667, declared that baptism did not change a person's enslaved or free condition: enslaved people could be baptized, but baptism could not secure their freedom.[3](93-notes.md#ch01-b03)

The acts addressed different questions. One determined how status passed on. The other specified a change that would not alter it. Together, they show that sustaining domination involves particular rules, directed both at birth and at routes people might later seek to change their situation.

For an enslaved person, entering a new religious community did not thereby confer legal standing to leave an owner. For the owner, baptism could not be a legal ground for losing that power. Viewed from its two ends, the same rule restricted a route to freedom and protected an existing entitlement.

This also helps explain why opposition to privilege cannot rest entirely on hoping that those with power will become kinder. An owner might behave with relative generosity while the institution permitting them to hold another person in slavery remained intact. As long as that status was protected, the person subjected to it had to depend on the attitude of someone entitled to decide their fate.

Institutional change requires a further step: turning what depends on another's permission into a right one can claim. A right matters because, even when the other party does not wish to grant it, the claimant has standing to demand it and to invoke rules applying to both parties.

Interests also give this relationship a reason to endure. If a rule gives one party continuing access to another's labour and places the labourer's children within the same arrangement, abolishing it means the beneficiary loses future labour and control. Preserving the rule therefore offers a foreseeable benefit. An enslaved person's demand for freedom for themselves and their children asks the beneficiary to surrender that benefit. The institution itself creates this conflict; it exists before any particular owner behaves with exceptional cruelty.

Understanding that incentive does not require imagining all beneficiaries as permanently united. They may compete or come into conflict. For people subjected to their power, the more immediate question is whether those conflicts change their rights or merely change who exercises control over them.

## After equality is declared

Article 1 of France's 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen proclaimed freedom and equality in rights. Article 6 required equal application of law and equal access to public office on grounds of capacity, virtue and talent. Article 1 also permitted social distinctions founded on public benefit.[4](93-notes.md#ch01-b04)

Such principles put power in a position of having to give reasons. Why someone should hold an office, or why a distinction should remain, required an answer beyond an assertion of status. Declaring equality supplied people facing exclusion with a language for judging institutions.

But principles must pass through specific arrangements before determining who actually participates. France's constitution of 1791 required “active citizens” participating in primary electoral assemblies to meet conditions including a minimum age of twenty-five, payment of direct tax worth at least three days' labour, and not being a hired domestic servant. The primary assemblies in turn selected electors.[5](93-notes.md#ch01-b05)

For someone excluded by the tax threshold or domestic-servant status, the consequence was concrete: they could not take part in choosing electors through these primary assemblies, yet still had to live under the resulting authority. Eligibility determined whose consent the institution counted.

If those setting the threshold for participation already stand inside it, they may give greater weight to the judgment of people like themselves and more readily explain exclusion as necessary for order. Those outside have less power to change the threshold through that same procedure. The danger is that setting the qualifications and enjoying them reinforce each other, giving a temporary distinction conditions in which to persist.

The declaration of equality mattered precisely because it gave those outside a standard they could invoke publicly. If freedom and rights belong to human beings, those excluding others should have to give reasons, rather than requiring the excluded endlessly to prove themselves worthy of admission. Whether that standard becomes an effective constraint also depends on people's ability to join together to express their views, challenge rules and bring about change.

If every criticism is answered with a demand not to question the victors, equality's promise will struggle to constrain those in power. Winning government and establishing institutions capable of limiting the winners are separate tasks. Success in the first does not automatically accomplish the second.

This book's examination of communism and CCP rule will continue along that line of inquiry. An organization committed to ending oppression should explain how it prevents its representatives from acquiring new powers of domination. An organization invoking the people's interests should explain how the people can test its claim to represent them. Specific histories must answer these questions.

## Examine how the power works

The preceding cases give us a way to look. Begin with the source of authority. Does someone's power over another come from birth, legal status, professional responsibilities or an authorization that can be scrutinized and changed? Different sources entail different responsibilities; the grandeur of a title does not settle the matter.

Then examine the scope of authority. Societies need people performing different tasks and procedures for reaching decisions. A doctor's professional judgment, a judge's adjudicative role and a public body's administrative decisions all require defined boundaries. A responsibility may entail particular powers without becoming a licence to control someone else's life at will.

Third, consider whether affected people can challenge a decision. Rules can be mistaken, and so can those applying them. Whether someone can obtain reasons, submit evidence, secure independent review and raise a good-faith objection without retaliation affects whether written rights provide practical protection.

Fourth, ask who bears the cost. If some people can decide others' lives while routinely shifting away the losses caused by their mistakes, they face less pressure to correct their decisions. Investigating a policy means identifying beneficiaries, those bearing its consequences, and the responsibilities connecting them.

These questions also help distinguish family life from hereditary domination. Parents pass knowledge, care or property to their children, raising a range of moral and institutional questions. If what passes also includes control over public appointments, the ability to exclude competitors or immunity from accountability, we must examine how institutions protect that advantage. The decisive issue is what it enables one person to do to another.

Freedom does not mean that anyone who dislikes a public rule may disregard every obligation. It requires rules with defensible grounds, boundaries on authority, and ways for those bearing obligations to challenge mistakes and bring about change. Obedience and accountability must be considered within the same institutional framework.

People's positions can overlap, institutions can change, and resisters can become new beneficiaries. Judgment must therefore follow the relationship of power. Someone who demanded release from domination yesterday should face the same constraints if they acquire power over others today.

## From being governed to giving authority

No child should become another person's property because of the family into which they are born. That is this book's liberal starting point: personal liberty should come before anyone's demand for obedience. It leads to an inquiry into how people acquire the power to determine their common life and prevent a few from possessing that power permanently.

The American Declaration of Independence of 1776 connected government's just powers to the consent of the governed. It asserted the people's right to alter or abolish a government that became destructive of the purposes it was meant to serve.[6](93-notes.md#ch01-b06) Authorization thereby stood at the centre of the political claim.

The change from assigned birth status to a principle of authorization concerns whom power must answer to. Where rule rests on status alone, the governed must first explain why they should be permitted to object. Where power requires authorization from the people, those exercising it must explain their grounds and limits.

How consent is expressed, who can participate, how persistent disagreement is handled, and how a new government is prevented from rebuilding old forms of domination all require practical answers. A new state proclaiming liberty must itself be examined against the standard it proposes.

The next chapter returns to American independence, exploring how that political claim emerged and whom it then encompassed. We will continue with the same question: When a power says it exists for the people, how can the people decide its fate?
