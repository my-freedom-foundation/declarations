# Chapter Twelve　What Freedom Requires

*In the People's Name: Privilege, Revolution, and Unaccountable Power · English draft v0.2 · 17 September 2026*

## When dissenters can carry on with their lives

This book about privilege ends by returning to someone who has none. He knows no minister, has no reputation likely to attract the media, and cannot pay for a long legal battle on his own. When he disagrees with the government, refuses to join an organization, or points out that someone powerful is harming others, can he still work, care for his family, and demand that someone address the matter?

Here freedom acquires a testable measure. Good treatment for people whom the rulers like tells us little. Those who most need protection are often the very people who make the powerful uncomfortable. This person may speak harshly, make errors of judgment, and disagree with us about many things. If asking a question costs him his livelihood, or getting an answer requires him to prove his political loyalty first, equality on paper has yet to become a right he can use.

Jourdon Anderson, whose letter we examined in Chapter Three, gave these rights a concrete form. Responding in 1865 to his former enslaver's invitation to return, he asked about wages for his past work and whether his children could live safely and receive an education. His demands do not establish that he actually received compensation. They do, however, bring freedom down to a human scale: whose labor is it, who gets to test a promise, and who decides whether to return?[1](93-notes.md#ch12-l01)

From inherited status to political consent, from slavery to revolution, and from power within the Party to interests across borders, this book has pursued the same question: can the represented change the decisions of those who represent them? Noble birth cannot answer for them. Revolutionary sacrifice cannot answer for them. Economic growth, professional reputation, and national security cannot permanently take the place of their voices either.

Opposition to the CCP's arbitrary one-party rule must follow this question through. Once a ruling group's monopoly over public power has ended, ordinary people still need ways to constrain its successors. Otherwise, the victors simply occupy their predecessors' positions, and the people wait for a new master's favors. What we must seek is the ability to refuse, question, and correct those in power—an ability that no ruler can easily take away.

## Individual rights come before political loyalty

No one should have to prove that they are useful, patriotic, or on the right side before qualifying for protection against arbitrary detention and humiliation. Nor should anyone have to accept a religion to receive equal protection of the law. People may differ in wealth, ability, and influence. Those differences must not become a hierarchy that determines who can harm others without answering for it.

The 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights brings equality before the law, effective remedies, an independent and impartial hearing, and freedoms of opinion, expression, peaceful assembly, and association into the same text. Freedom of association includes protection against being compelled to join an association. These provisions offer a shared normative standard; effective protection still requires procedures and institutions that people can actually use.[2](93-notes.md#ch12-l02)

Freedom from compulsory membership deserves particular attention. When an organization controls career opportunities, public resources, and how people are judged after leaving, formally voluntary membership can involve enormous pressure. Education, basic public services, and ordinary professional activity should therefore not depend on support for the governing organization. Criticizing the government must not cost someone's children their access to school. Leaving a political organization must not exclude a person from normal life.

Rights must also withstand the majority's impatience. A majority may decide policy under shared rules, but it cannot strip a minority of the right to defend itself or seek redress merely because its members are irritating, awkward, or stubborn. The minority treated as an obstacle to progress today may be the first to recognize danger tomorrow. Even if its members remain wrong, basic rights should never be a prize for correct judgment.

Speech and conduct can, of course, harm others. Violence, threats, and fraud require restraint. Restrictions must nevertheless identify the specific conduct, actual risk, and legal basis involved, and remain open to independent review. Renaming criticism as harm, or a ruler's displeasure as danger, places every right back under the ruler's interpretation.

A person's freedom also depends on whether exercising a right is affordable. Legal aid, accessible complaint procedures, and prompt action against retaliation can give people without money or connections room to act. When resources are limited, the scope and priorities of such services need public discussion. Poverty must not become a reason for power to refuse an answer.

## Authorization must be revocable

People authorize government to address shared concerns that individuals cannot handle alone. That authority has limits of scope and duration. Support received in the past does not amount to consent for every future decision. Nor can a single victory entitle a family or organization to represent the people forever.

Article 25 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights connects citizens' opportunities to take part in public affairs, vote, and stand for election with genuine periodic elections. Elections must express the freely formed will of the voters. This principle requires a meaningful choice; it does not require a change of officeholders at every election.[3](93-notes.md#ch12-l03)

Meaningful choice depends on the whole stretch of public life before and after the vote. Whether candidates can offer different policies, opposition groups can organize, journalists can investigate those in office, and citizens can choose without retaliation all affect what a ballot represents. If incumbents first decide who may oppose them, then determine which criticisms may circulate, and finally preside over a contest in which only they can win, authorization can no longer be withdrawn.

A transfer of power must also extend beyond announcing the result. Those who lose an election should relinquish the powers they no longer lawfully hold. Administrative officials should perform their duties under public rules, while the armed forces and police should follow lawful public command rather than the private will of a party or individual. Changing officeholders must change who makes decisions while allowing public services to continue. Ordinary people should not have to choose between submitting to the old rulers and seeing their lives collapse every time they replace a government.

This is also why limiting power requires protection for those who lose. Losing an election, criticizing the government, or leaving an organization should not in itself be grounds for persecution. Holding people legally accountable for wrongdoing is compatible with allowing those defeated in politics to live safely as ordinary citizens. If leaving office means that an entire family will inevitably face retribution, rulers have a stronger incentive to resist departure at any cost.

Fundamental change to CCP rule should include removing every party's standing above people's rights and the law that applies to everyone. A party may seek support, but it cannot claim the state in advance. A political idea may be advocated and challenged, but it cannot replace citizens' continuing right to choose. Those who advocate freedom must also accept that their proposals may be rejected and their representatives replaced.

## The highest power must face someone entitled to say no

The 1974 Nixon case in the United States brought limits on the highest office to bear on specific evidence. The Special Prosecutor sought recordings and documents relating to particular presidential conversations; the President moved to quash the subpoena. The dispute concerned more than what the public wished to know. It asked whether a general claim of presidential confidentiality could block a demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a criminal trial.[4](93-notes.md#ch12-l04)

The Supreme Court recognized the interest in confidential communications between a president and his advisers, but did not accept that it outweighed the need for evidence in this case. The material was to go first to the District Court for private review, with irrelevant or inadmissible material protected. The judgment did not abolish all presidential privilege or resolve every question about congressional demands for evidence, state secrets, and related matters. It showed that, at least in this particular dispute, the President could not decide alone whether a demand for evidence from him was valid.[4](93-notes.md#ch12-l04)

That boundary matters. If an investigation can obtain only what its subject chooses to provide, the highest authority still controls the limits of its own scrutiny. Power truly faces someone able to say no only when others can lawfully require material, examine reasons for confidentiality, and issue an effective decision. Secrecy may remain necessary. But the same party cannot forever control who decides that necessity, how records are kept, and when the decision is reviewed.

Independent adjudication requires everyday support. Judges must not lose their positions for decisions that displease their superiors; courts must not lose essential resources for hearing sensitive cases; parties must not be punished for hiring lawyers. The UN's 1985 Basic Principles on the Independence of the Judiciary address freedom from improper interference, adequate resources, qualifications, tenure, and disciplinary procedures. These principles do not prove that a world court has already secured such protections for every country. They provide concrete reference points for judging whether independence can work.[5](93-notes.md#ch12-l05)

Judgment must be followed by enforcement. If a government can lose a case and indefinitely refuse to comply, an ordinary person has gained only a sheet of paper bearing their name. Article 2(3) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights requires effective remedies and enforcement of remedies that have been granted.[3](93-notes.md#ch12-l03) For someone whose rights have been violated, overturning a wrongful decision, lifting an unjustified restriction, or receiving compensation that is due has a more direct effect than an institution's willingness to admit that it chose its words poorly.

Independence must not turn judges into a new untouchable group. Decisions need reasons, conflicts of interest require recusal, errors need appropriate avenues of review, and corruption and serious dereliction of duty should be addressed under law. Protecting judicial independence means freeing decisions from improper orders while holding those who exercise judicial power responsible to law and procedure.

## Money, appointments, and force must answer to the public

The way a policy changes people's lives is often buried in appropriations, appointments, procurement, and orders for implementation. If the public sees only the final publicity and cannot question these processes, formal access to information remains narrow. Who receives a contract, who decides whom to hire, and who benefits from an exception determine whether public resources are becoming the private assets of a few.

The United Nations Convention against Corruption addresses transparency in political financing, conflicts of interest, officials' declarations of interests, public procurement, and financial management. Its provisions impose obligations of differing strength and retain conditions concerning relevant principles of domestic law.[6](93-notes.md#ch12-l06) The text identifies matters that can be questioned; it does not automatically expose hidden relationships.

Useful disclosure should allow a person to follow a decision and keep asking questions. A budget explains what is to be done; spending records show where the money went; procurement reasons explain why a particular supplier was chosen. Major changes, exemptions, and conflicts of interest also require explanations that can be examined. The public need not acquire details of every ordinary employee's private life. It should, however, be able to discover whether someone exercising public power is allocating benefits to themselves or connected parties.

Political financing and benefits received after leaving office also need scrutiny. Donors must not acquire a secret veto by paying, and officials must not exchange favorable treatment of an industry while in office for concealed rewards afterward. The precise scope of disclosure, recusal requirements, and restrictions after leaving office should be established publicly, balancing ordinary employment with the prevention of improper exchanges. We cannot investigate only our opponents' connections while describing our allies' connections as friendship.

Anticorruption bodies themselves must face scrutiny. Their investigative and coercive powers exist to establish conduct; they must not become tools through which leaders select enemies and redistribute loyalty. How subjects are selected, whether measures are necessary, why property is restricted, and how those affected can object all require a basis that can be traced. Publicizing a few sensational cases cannot replace an explanation of selective enforcement.

Force, above all, must not become a circle's private possession. The armed forces, police, and intelligence agencies need legally defined duties, records of important orders, and independent oversight with authority to obtain necessary material. Sensitive operations may require some confidentiality, but funding, authorization, and responsibility for unlawful conduct must still be examined. Personnel carrying out orders should have channels for reporting manifestly unlawful commands and seeking protection. Their responsibility should be assessed in light of their actual authority, conduct, and circumstances.

These constraints must make honest performance of duty a sustainable choice. Punishing only errors by personnel at the bottom, without asking how superiors set targets, concealed information, and rewarded or punished subordinates, pushes responsibility onto those most easily replaced. Following resources and orders upward allows those who make decisions to bear responsibility proportionate to their power.

## Emergency powers must come to an end

War, disasters, and major epidemics compress the time available for decisions. Governments may need to mobilize resources quickly, and individuals may have to accept temporary restrictions. The less time there is for full discussion, however, the more necessary it becomes to establish in advance who can approve a measure, which reasons must be recorded, how long it may last, and who can demand reconsideration.

Article 4 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights places emergency derogations under strict conditions. A public emergency threatening the life of the nation must be officially proclaimed; measures must be limited to what the situation strictly requires; and the article includes rights from which no derogation is permitted, along with notification requirements. It does not itself prescribe a uniform set of fixed sunset periods.[3](93-notes.md#ch12-l03)

Building on that foundation, every emergency authorization that expands coercive power should have clear conditions for expiry. An extension should require a renewed explanation of the risks, results achieved, burdens imposed, and feasible alternatives, reviewed by someone with authority to refuse it. A continuing risk does not establish that every restriction initially adopted remains necessary.

Emergency procurement, restrictions on individuals, and large-scale data collection should also leave records that can be examined afterward. Information temporarily obtained to address a danger must not quietly become a means of controlling dissent once that danger has passed. Conditions for retaining, deleting, or repurposing data after the authority expires need to be explained in advance, with independent oversight of compliance.

Reasonableness also depends on who bears the burden. The same restriction may affect someone able to work from home very differently from someone living on each day's earnings. Compensation, services, and avenues of appeal must form part of the decision itself. Authorities must not wait until harm occurs and then treat everyone unable to comply as unwilling to cooperate.

Swift action and scrutiny can support each other. Traceable orders tell those implementing them where the limits lie. Clear conditions for expiry tell the public when it can seek the lifting of restrictions. Prompt correction makes it easier for necessary measures to earn trust. An emergency must never become a reason for anyone to keep additional powers permanently.

## Give victims answers and set limits on accountability

Changing an oppressive political order does not automatically restore the lives its victims lost. Archives may remain closed, false records may still affect employment and families, injuries and illness require care, and relatives of the missing continue to wait for news. If new rulers simply tell everyone to look ahead, those who suffered may again be asked to stay silent for someone else's grand objective.

The basic principles on remedy and reparation adopted by the UN General Assembly in 2005 address victims' access to information, remedies, and reparation for gross human rights violations and serious violations of humanitarian law. The text covers protection against intimidation and retaliation before, during and after proceedings, access to relevant information, restitution, compensation, rehabilitation, and prevention of recurrence. It also expressly states that it creates no new international or domestic legal obligations.[7](93-notes.md#ch12-l07)

These requirements mean that accountability cannot be measured simply by how many people are punished. Whether victims know how an investigation is progressing, can make requests, receive reasoned decisions, and actually obtain compensation should all be open to public examination. Disclosure must also take account of the safety of witnesses and families. Investigators must not demonstrate their own courage by handing the risk back to those who supplied the material.

Responsibility must be traced to organizational decisions and individual conduct. Who designed a policy, controlled its funding, continued implementing it despite knowing the harm, concealed records, or benefited from the outcome must be established separately. Explanations at the organizational level can guide an investigation. Treating every member, relative, or person of the same nationality as a criminal dissolves specific responsibility into collective hatred.

Procedural protections apply to the accused as well. Access to a defense, knowledge of the allegations and evidence, and judgment by an independent body help prevent power from again inflicting arbitrary harm. Victims' anger has every reason to be heard, but anger cannot replace establishing what each person actually did. Otherwise, new rulers need only rename the enemy to keep using the old methods of persecution.

A previous position may make someone unsuitable to continue exercising a particular public power. That, too, requires public standards, individual review, and an opportunity to appeal. Preserving archives, maintaining basic services, and preventing relevant personnel from continuing to interfere with investigations can proceed alongside lawful accountability. Society need not accept blanket immunity to restore ordinary life, nor permanently exclude everyone who once worked in the old system in order to pursue responsibility.

Preventing recurrence ultimately requires changing the conditions that made harm possible. Why could wrongful orders be carried out all the way down? Why was there no effective route for a plea for help? Why could courts not intervene, and why did the public remain uninformed for so long? These questions need answers. If individuals are punished while those conditions remain, their successors may still use the same powers.

## Those who scrutinize others must accept scrutiny

Journalists, scholars, public-interest organizations, and technology platforms can help ordinary people obtain information that would otherwise be beyond their reach. They can also control new points of access: deciding who may publish, which material deserves attention, and which questions are treated as settled. A history of opposing a powerful institution does not automatically prevent abuse of this gatekeeping power.

Those who scrutinize others should therefore explain their methods, disclose significant interests affecting their judgment, keep correction records, and offer those affected a way to respond. Funding must not carry a secret right to suppress an investigation, and professional credentials must not excuse a refusal to explain reasoning. For sources who cannot be identified publicly, protecting their identities and explaining the verification process are separate responsibilities.

The technology platform discussed in Chapter Eleven should meet these requirements from the outset. Its founders' sound initial motives cannot entitle them to decide forever which material deserves to exist. Independent review, traceable revisions, appropriate data export, and limits on access and decision-making powers should allow participants to challenge the platform itself. When one point of access fails, the material and the work should not all vanish with it.

These requirements also govern the writing of this book. Material supporting a judgment needs an account of its sources' opportunity to know, its actual content, and the connections among its sources. Material challenging that judgment deserves proportionate examination as well. Different sources should carry different weight, but the reasons for assigning that weight must remain open to discussion. Agreement with us cannot make anyone permanently reliable; disagreement cannot automatically make them subject to someone else's control.

When considering people making disclosures, including Dr. Li-Meng Yan, we can respect their professional experience, take their material seriously, and oppose improper suppression while examining specific judgments. Protecting someone's ability to speak does not require guaranteeing in advance that everything they say is correct. Revising a judgment must not become a reason to retaliate against the person who spoke. Keeping those responsibilities separate allows honest people to speak without staking their whole lives on never making a mistake.

One of the strongest tests of the commitment of those who stand for freedom comes when a claim favorable to their own side needs correction. The ability to correct it publicly without punishing whoever identified the problem shows that the search for truth matters more than preserving prestige. Any organization that rejects this requirement leaves room for new privilege.

## International cooperation must answer to ordinary people

Earlier chapters have shown why relationships of interest must be traced through specific funding, access, promises, and conduct. The same applies when countries act together: shared slogans cannot exempt partners from scrutiny. A government's declaration of support for freedom does not mean every transaction it makes protects freedom. Nor can an alliance replace scrutiny of one's own government's power.

International cooperation should carry responsibilities that can be checked. Information concerning public safety needs channels for prompt notification and independent review. Research cooperation needs an explanation of funding and authority. Major public transactions must withstand examination for conflicts of interest. Credible people making disclosures from societies under authoritarian rule should have an opportunity to receive protection and substantive review, rather than being turned away merely because they are diplomatically inconvenient.

In response to serious abuses, investigation, preservation of evidence, lawful accountability, and support for victims can sustain action over time. Restrictive measures should identify the conduct they target, the results they seek, and when they will be reassessed. Passing costs indiscriminately to ordinary families may not change the incentives of those who actually make decisions. The effects of such measures need examination; they cannot be established by the resolve proclaimed by those imposing them.

Chinese people's language, ancestry, and birthplace do not make them the property of the CCP. Immigrants, international students, and ordinary businesspeople must not bear collective guilt for a regime's conduct. Actual coercion, agency relationships, and unlawful acts should instead be investigated through specific evidence. A free society must protect everyone's equal standing if it is to demand that other regimes stop using identity to dominate individuals.

Resisting expansion and coercion requires capacity, along with the public's ability to question how that capacity is used. Every decision to resort to force makes particular people bear its costs. Claims of historical inevitability cannot replace an explanation of objectives, necessity, consequences, and responsibility. The pursuit of freedom does not need a predetermined final war as its guarantee. Enabling more people to say no safely is already an undertaking that demands continuing work.

## Turn the declaration into commitments that can be fulfilled

The current public text of the 2022 Liberty Declaration begins with equal rights. It explains that the powers of councils, governments, courts, and armies come from collective authorization, and calls for the withdrawal of that authorization and the choice of a new government when violations of rights persist. Both its Chinese and English conclusions make protection of every person's basic rights a goal of political reconstruction.[8](93-notes.md#ch12-l08)

This book develops that demand further. The original declaration preserves the positions and allegations it expressed at the time. Specific historical allegations still need to be checked against the relevant material; their inclusion in a declaration cannot exempt them from correction. Completing a book will not cause missing evidence to appear. Principles of freedom should withstand revisions of fact without depending on every original formulation remaining correct forever.

These principles must now become requirements for those who act, including everyone who declares opposition to the CCP and seeks to build a free political order. The commitments that follow are this book's further proposals:

**Everyone has equal basic rights.** Faith may sustain a person's pursuit of freedom, but public rights must not depend on accepting the same faith. Political dissent, family background, ethnicity, and nationality must never be grounds for arbitrarily denying someone's dignity or due process.

**Every representative must be replaceable.** Allow genuine competition, accept independent scrutiny, explain major decisions publicly, and lawfully relinquish powers no longer held. Sacrifice, achievement, and victory cannot purchase a permanent right to rule.

**Factual claims must remain open to examination and correction.** Protect truthful disclosures, investigate fabrication, and preserve the record of how judgments change. No institution, leader, expert, or news organization can acquire in advance a standing beyond all question.

**Responsibility must follow actual conduct.** Investigate how organizations issue orders, supply resources, and administer rewards and punishments, while establishing individual responsibility separately. No person's guilt may be passed to their family, and no party's responsibility may be assigned to an entire people.

**Peaceful correction must become a usable means of change.** Expression, association, litigation, scrutiny, and changes of government should be capable of changing outcomes. Emergency powers need time limits and review, victims need remedies, and those exercising oversight must themselves accept scrutiny.

**Test these commitments against ordinary people's actual circumstances.** When someone without a reputation is harmed, their ability to find a way to raise the matter, receive an answer, obtain protection, and bring about correction determines whether rights have moved beyond paper. A new government's praise of itself cannot make that judgment for the people.

These commitments do not require everyone to accept the same political philosophy. They require people with different beliefs to share certain limits that the strong cannot arbitrarily erase. Only then can opposition to the CCP's arbitrary power lead to ordinary people's freedom, carrying that purpose beyond the moment when a different group takes power.

## Freedom must hold for people who disagree with us

At the end, we can return to the person without a powerful backer whom we met at the beginning. He may support us, or he may thoroughly dislike this book. Either way, he should be able to demand reasons from the government, require those with power to stop causing harm, and, when necessary, join with others to change a decision.

We need not wait for everyone to become virtuous before beginning to constrain power. Rules are needed precisely because they must work when people are greedy, fearful, vain, or unwilling to admit error. The courage of good people deserves to be valued. Ordinary people's safety cannot depend solely on good people happening to occupy crucial positions.

Freedom will not be delivered once and for all at a particular moment in history. Laws may be hollowed out, oversight bought off, and opponents of power unwilling to face opposition after they succeed. Every generation must examine anew who controls decisions, who bears the consequences, who can object, and who has the right to demand correction.

This work does not require everyone to become a hero. Preserving a record that might be erased, supporting someone facing retaliation, demanding an explanation for an exception granted by those in power, and insisting on correction when one's own side makes a mistake can all reduce the space in which power evades responsibility. Circumstances differ, as do the risks individuals can bear. Courage must not become another unreasonable demand on the oppressed.

We oppose any organization that treats the state as its property and any victor who treats the people as its dependants. Representing the people must include the possibility that they will cease to accept that representation. Protecting the people must allow them to scrutinize their protectors' power.

Power exercised in the people's name must be brought back within bounds that allow the people to refuse it, correct it, and take it back.
