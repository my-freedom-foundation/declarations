# Chapter Ten　Who Decides What the Public Knows?

*In the People's Name: Privilege, Revolution, and Unaccountable Power · English draft v0.6 · 17 September 2026*

## How a warning becomes an admonition

The previous chapter asked who can make participants in an exchange bear costs transferred to others. Accountability has an earlier gateway: can the public learn what happened? If a decision's records are concealed and the first people to recognize a problem fear speaking, others may struggle to identify the danger, whom to question, or what needs to change.

On 30 December 2019, Wuhan doctor Li Wenliang shared a message about seven cases of “SARS,” along with related material, in a WeChat group of former classmates. Later that evening, he added that the virus was still being typed and advised precautions among relatives and friends. Police admonished him on 3 January 2020. The National Supervisory Commission's subsequent investigation report also recorded that his hospital had questioned him and required a written reflection before the police meeting.[1](93-notes.md#ch10-t01)

The information passed through two kinds of judgment. Medical questions concerned what had been observed, the extent of the danger, and what needed verification. Administrative questions concerned permission to speak, the effects of circulation, and acceptance of a warning from authorities. When the second judgment brings personal consequences first, it can constrict the space in which the first inquiry might develop.

Warnings usually arise before knowledge is complete. Li's initial disease label needed correction; he also supplied a qualification. A warning's weight depends on its underlying material, the danger identified, and the details open to verification. One person with specific information may be closer to the problem than many repeating an authorized account. Rank and numbers cannot replace access to knowledge. A protective system should allow precaution and verification to proceed together, without demanding the complete answer available only in retrospect.

If waiting avoids professional and administrative risks while speaking invites admonition, silence becomes personally safer. That calculation may serve the speaker's self-preservation while imposing heavy costs on others still exposed to danger. Suppression of warnings becomes especially dangerous when individual survival incentives conflict with public need.

## After reversal, who explains what happened?

In a Caixin interview, Li said he feared being unable to leave if he refused to sign the admonition and worried that hospital punishment would affect future promotion. These were his descriptions of the pressure he faced. On the place of differing views, he said a healthy society should not have only one voice.[2](93-notes.md#ch10-t02)

His observation carries the question beyond one person's experience to the choices available to others. A doctor considers more than whether an assessment is correct. He must also consider who influences his employment, standing, and future. When people controlling professional and administrative resources determine the price of speaking, many may learn to ask whether speech is safe without any order explicitly demanding universal silence.

On 19 March 2020, the National Supervisory Commission's investigation team found the admonition improper and the procedures irregular, recommending its withdrawal. Wuhan police subsequently decided to revoke it and apologized to Li's family. Li had already died on 7 February.[1](93-notes.md#ch10-t01)

Correction matters, but a late reversal cannot restore the choices available to someone at the time. To protect others, it must lead to questions about why a warning was treated that way, whether similar decisions can recur, and how someone being questioned can obtain timely help. Rejecting an individual action leaves the incentives behind it to be changed.

The investigation team's questions and answers that day acknowledged Li's intention to warn classmates and the contribution his information made to greater awareness of the outbreak. Its final passage also attributed portrayals of him as an opponent of the political system to hostile forces.[1](93-notes.md#ch10-t01) The text corrected the admonition while attempting to prescribe the event's political meaning.

People should be free to discuss the institutional reasons behind such treatment. An institution may acknowledge misconduct while still requiring acceptance of a designated explanation. The authority to correct and the authority to interpret can remain concentrated on the same side. Meaningful change should allow critics to pursue unwelcome questions without first establishing an acceptable political identity.

## Whom must the news answer to first?

Information's boundaries change with the people an institution must answer to. Reporters need access and opportunities to publish; editors need to sustain their organizations. Whoever influences those conditions may affect the selection and presentation of stories. The dependence examined in the previous chapter now concerns whether information reaches the public.

On 19 February 2016, Xi Jinping told a symposium on the Party's news and public-opinion work to put political direction first. Xinhua's account recorded his requirement that Party- and government-run media serve the Party, extending requirements of direction across different media and fields of coverage. The speech also called for truthful reporting, accurate criticism, and scrutiny through public opinion.[3](93-notes.md#ch10-t03)

Taken together, these demands reveal where scrutiny is situated. Media may expose some problems, while the legitimacy of criticism remains interpreted within Party leadership. When criticism reaches the highest decision-makers in that framework, whether journalists can persist becomes a crucial test of independent scrutiny.

This book opposes placing loyalty to a governing organization above public knowledge and independent oversight. Where reporting may affect a decision-maker's authority, prior obedience to that person's political direction creates a conflict before investigation begins. Accuracy requirements have value; their practical force depends on whether journalists can still publish when facts embarrass leaders.

This arrangement also changes the material available for public judgment. Ten outlets citing one announcement or consulting the same small group of experts need not provide ten independent checks. If dissent carries higher professional and publication costs, the visible distribution of opinion has been filtered. Treating the remaining voices as a majority reached through free discussion can mistake the effects of control for the strength of the facts.

A claimed consensus therefore needs examination of its formation: whether participants obtained material independently, tested it substantively, and could publish differing results. Converging independent research can increase confidence; repetition cannot accumulate weight merely by adding names. Sources should receive weight according to these differences. An institutional statement and an insider's specific evidence do not each simply receive one vote.

Press freedom therefore cannot be measured solely by counting outlets or daily stories. Important questions must be open to independent inquiry, relevant material accessible, and critics' careers protected from the power of those criticized. People need media capable of investigating power, including where power would prefer no investigation.

## How platforms turn requirements into routine choices

Online visibility also depends on review, ranking, and recommendation. Removal makes content disappear; prominent placement makes other content more likely to reach readers. Decisions about what can remain and what should be brought forward can shape a society's understanding of itself.

The Cyberspace Administration of China's provisions on governing the online information content ecosystem, published in 2019 and effective on 1 March 2020, incorporated such requirements into platform operations. Articles 8–10 assign governance responsibilities, require review and inspection systems, and mandate action, recordkeeping, and reporting concerning specified content. Article 11 encourages prominent placement of designated material, including promotion of Party ideas and policies. Article 12 imposes requirements concerning recommendation models and human intervention on platforms using algorithmic recommendations.[4](93-notes.md#ch10-t04)

These provisions took effect after Li's admonition and were not its legal basis. They offer another vantage point: political direction entering the daily work of editing, review, and recommendation through platform responsibilities. Operators need not await individual instructions about every item to know which consequences they must bear.

Under those conditions, operators may regard removing too much as safer than removing too little. Authors may adjust expression in advance to reduce the risk of rejection or action against an account. This is an inference from the distribution of responsibility; its actual extent requires evidence of implementation. It helps explain how control can extend beyond item-by-item commands.

Fraud, harassment, and false information do exist online. The questions concern clear reasons for action, consistent application, timely appeal, and whether political criticism is folded into risks to be suppressed. Leaving every dispute to judgments that cannot be independently challenged entangles correction of mistakes with rulers' convenience.

An abundance of information does not guarantee circulation of the information that matters. Vast quantities of innocuous material may remain while a few questions about power, responsibility, and collective action are suppressed. The visible world may be lively yet lack elements essential to political judgment.

Pressure can also travel through overseas companies. In December 2020, Zoom acknowledged negotiating corrective measures with Chinese officials to restore service after its blockage in China in 2019. Its updated June statement also acknowledged terminating meetings and suspending some overseas hosts' accounts after Chinese government demands concerning Tiananmen commemorations.[13](93-notes.md#ch10-t13) The company's admissions provide specific demands and actions, carrying more weight than an inference drawn solely from its operations in China.

Market access became a means of influencing platform decisions, while account permissions carried pressure beyond China's borders. Overseas registration cannot guarantee independence. Investigation of another institution should trace the same connections: the interests at risk, who makes requests, who can execute them, and how conduct changes. Nationality and company branding cannot substitute for that relationship.

## How expert judgment enters a story

Professional standing and personal connections also influence opportunities to be heard. Journalists need explanations of complex matters; researchers offer assessments, contacts, and context. Cooperation can help the public understand science. When the expert is also involved in a controversy, editors need to understand that person's positions, interests, and limits of knowledge.

Text and screenshots published by Lude in July 2026 brought private scientific correspondence about Dr. Li-Meng Yan's reports to readers' attention. Following that lead reaches Slack material released by the Senate, including surrounding conversations. Here the question concerns discussion and dissemination. The next chapter uses additional records to examine the attention the reports received and how institutions should respond to early warnings.[5](93-notes.md#ch10-t05)

In an exchange dated 25–26 September 2020, Eddie Holmes discussed contact with New York Times reporter Amy Qin and welcomed what he understood as an effort to use reporting to discredit Dr. Yan's report. The characterization of the reporter's intention was Holmes's. Messages in November again included a relevant story link and Holmes's statement that the reporter had emailed him.[6](93-notes.md#ch10-t06)

These messages carry different weight for different questions. Holmes's descriptions of his own contacts and desired effects are more direct than outsiders' guesses about his position; his characterization of the reporter's intentions remains secondhand. The material already warrants renewed scrutiny of independence between expert advice and reporting. A source involved in a controversy may provide knowledge while promoting an account favorable to himself. Complete editorial records could test that explanation further; they are not the sole ticket to beginning a judgment.

Nearby conversations also show these researchers criticizing errors in an article rebutting Dr. Yan and calling for China to be more open.[6](93-notes.md#ch10-t06) These statements preserve the complexity of their positions without cancelling the preceding contacts. Criticizing some Chinese conduct cannot establish freedom from interests or partiality in another dispute.

Financial relationships also require specific examination. The New York Times Company's 2020 proxy statement shows a family trust controlling the election of a majority of directors through Class B shares. A separate China Daily FARA filing lists a $50,000 advertising payment to the Times in November 2018.[14](93-notes.md#ch10-t14) The first establishes a formal control structure; the second records an actual commercial transaction. They warrant inquiry into family power, advertising interests, and editorial independence, but have not been connected to payment or editorial instructions for the 2020 story about Dr. Yan.

Cooperation need not turn its participants into members of one organization. Research funding, institutional reputation, market access, or a policy objective may lead people with different positions to help each other on one matter while disputing others. Such cooperation can affect what the public sees. Establishing it involves comparing conduct before and after contact, specific requests, and exchanges of benefits. Benefits identify motives to examine; benefiting alone does not establish joint planning of an event.

The CCP's united front regulations revised in 2020 make handling unity and diversity and accommodating allies' interests principles of its work. Its constituency is people outside the Party, with particular emphasis on representative figures among them.[16](93-notes.md#ch10-t16) The arrangement shows that extending Party influence does not require every participant to become a member first. Its incentive structure suggests that someone may accept cooperation for personal objectives while retaining other disagreements. Information's independence therefore also depends on relationships and cooperation within a particular issue; a history of opposing the CCP cannot settle it. Specific implementation still needs assessment through the person's contacts and conduct.

Scientists may sharply criticize reports, and journalists may investigate their production and circulation. The public needs to assess whether criticism addresses the actual argument, whether reporting discloses relevant positions and allows a fair response, and whether reputation is being used to disqualify a question before examination. Judgments must concern specific contacts and conduct; a name or ethnicity supplies no evidence of control. Expert authority should bring readers closer to material, rather than confer exemption from explanation.

## When official records leave public view

Some barriers to information reach a more specific level of responsibility. In August 2026, Nuomt published an account of former National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases official David Morens's guilty plea and related material on the Lude website.[5](93-notes.md#ch10-t05) The case connects the previous chapter's cooperation around interests with control over information: collaboration involving funding and institutional relationships also affected which records the public could later obtain.

On 18 August 2026, the Justice Department announced that Morens had pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit offenses and to defraud the United States. His conduct involved hiding official correspondence about coronavirus research funding through private Gmail to evade disclosure and federal records requirements. The announcement and court stipulation of facts place the communications within efforts to restore funding, exchange nonpublic institutional information, and coordinate action.[7](93-notes.md#ch10-t07)

The stipulation he signed further records a public-facing purpose of concealment: omitting one co-conspirator from supporting correspondence and contributor lists for writings favoring the company concerned, to create an appearance of disinterested scientific consensus and avoid competing-interest disclosures.[7](93-notes.md#ch10-t07)

The admission also changes the weight due to the public support concerned. Apparently separate judgments may involve undisclosed common participation. For material actually affected by the arrangement, the number and standing of supporters deserve less evidentiary weight; shared sources and substantive arguments require renewed scrutiny. That adjustment need not wait until every scientific conclusion has also been overturned.

More was at stake than one requester's chance to read a few emails. Official records allow outsiders to determine who participated in a decision, in what capacity, with what reasons, and whether personal relationships influenced public resource allocation. Deliberately moving records outside systems intended to retain and retrieve them makes reconstruction harder.

Participants may understand cooperation as helping friends, protecting research, or correcting injustice. Those aims cannot turn official authority into a resource for private handling. The more beneficiaries value retaining funding and reputation, the more they may want less scrutiny. When a public official joins the arrangement, recordkeeping duties provide a practical limit on that convergence of interests.

Morens's plea supplies a basis for criminal responsibility concerning concealment. It does not automatically turn every research controversy into an adjudicated fact or convict others who have not been convicted. Further investigation should follow communications, responsibilities, and participants already identified, requiring each person to answer for their conduct.

The judgment of this book is explicit: taking part in decisions as a public official while deliberately depriving people of the material needed to examine them violates public trust. Conduct does not become an inconsequential internal communication habit because it occurs in a research institution or involves professional standing.

## Making records a usable right

Material not presently suitable for public release still needs preservation under applicable rules. Preservation and disclosure solve connected problems: the former leaves a trace of decision-making, and the latter gives entitled people access. If records are first destroyed or concealed, a later promise of openness may have nothing left to release.

National Archives guidance distinguishes relevant records made or received in conducting federal business from purely personal material. For federal records created or received in personal or nonofficial electronic accounts, it specifies copying an official account or forwarding a complete copy within twenty days.[8](93-notes.md#ch10-t08) Account ownership alone does not determine the public character of its contents.

The Freedom of Information Act supplies a route to request records from federal agencies, subject to specified exemptions including privacy and national security. Administrative appeals and judicial review can challenge improper withholding. Courts may order improperly withheld records produced, with the agency required to justify its refusal.[9](93-notes.md#ch10-t09)

These arrangements turn information from a favor into a claimable right. A requester need not first win the subject's trust to seek records of official business. Practical access nevertheless depends on adequate searches, timely responses, reviewable reasons for refusal, and the person's ability to bear the costs of a dispute.

Delay changes bargaining power. A requester may continue spending time and money while the record-holder maintains an existing account. If correction takes years, an erroneous narrative may first affect elections, policy, and reputations. Timeliness, independent review, and consequences for unlawful concealment make information rights effective.

Disclosure must also protect uninvolved individuals and specific legitimate interests. A public right to understand official business does not require limitless exposure of everyone's private correspondence. Restrictions should be explained in relation to particular content and remain open to independent examination. They must not become a blanket excuse to remove an entire decision-making process from scrutiny indefinitely.

## What remains after a hearing?

A congressional hearing can bring scattered records, questions, and responsible people into one public setting. It removes an institution's exclusive control over who may ask questions and may require those holding information to explain past conduct. Public questioning also allows more people to understand an investigation's direction.

On 29 July 2026, Anthony Fauci appeared before the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee. Related committee material records that, after an opening statement, he repeatedly invoked the Fifth Amendment in declining to answer questions.[10](93-notes.md#ch10-t10)

That refusal is a different kind of material from Morens's guilty plea. Constitutional protection against compelled self-incrimination can apply to testimony in congressional investigations. Its application to particular questions, including immunity and other legal conditions, requires legal determination. Invoking the right is not itself an admission of an accusation.[11](93-notes.md#ch10-t11)

Liberalism must protect a person's procedural rights while allowing criticism of that person. Otherwise, constraints discarded today to interrogate an official may tomorrow be discarded to force an ordinary critic to supply the state's preferred answer. Scrutiny of power must itself operate through constrained power.

Refusal does not resolve an investigation's questions. Investigators can continue examining records, comparing witnesses' accounts, tracing custody, and seeking further information through applicable procedures. An allegation embedded in a hearing question still requires supporting material. An unanswered question should remain recorded as unanswered.

The memorable part of a hearing may be its confrontation. Establishing responsibility often depends on subsequent work: completing a chronology, checking documents, identifying duties, and confronting participants with specific evidence. A hearing's political impact and its evidentiary progress both need to be assessed.

## When one account can outweigh many

When established institutions hold material and have reasons to evade responsibility, insiders and independent recordkeepers may offer an irreplaceable route inward. Material organized by Lude and Nuomt led this book to correspondence and case files. Its value concerns what it opened, what could be checked, and which connections remain available to pursue.

A credible insider can overturn many reports dependent on one authorized account. The more closed an information environment, the more a demand for prior institutional agreement allows those controlling access to decide who deserves belief. People need not wait for every institution to change its position before taking traceable records, informed testimony, and verified details seriously. Institutional statements, papers, news reports, programs, and written recaps all require examination for dependence on one information source.

Dr. Li-Meng Yan's experience deserves its weight under this standard. A SARS-CoV-2 hamster study published in 2020 lists her among its equally contributing authors and explicitly credits her with designing and performing experiments.[15](93-notes.md#ch10-t15) This establishes actual involvement more specifically than a qualification or an undifferentiated author list. Describing her as an outsider wholly unfamiliar with the field would conflict with that record. Her accounts of the research environment and information flow deserve investigation through her specific opportunities for access.

Her first report argues for an engineered origin through comparisons of viral sequences and features; her second further challenges some sequencing data and sample descriptions.[15](93-notes.md#ch10-t15) Those arguments contain material that can be checked and cannot be dismissed wholesale through institutional opposition. Explaining anomalies requires scrutiny of comparison groups, data quality, and premises. Technical feasibility and historical occurrence are two judgments that require an argument connecting them.

Criticism of the reports needs the same examination. A reviewer raises whole-genome differences and related features in other viruses; Dr. Yan's team responds that location and lineage matter.[15](93-notes.md#ch10-t15) Saying a feature occurs elsewhere may not answer a question about a particular location, while rarity among known samples cannot establish the impossibility of natural processes. The stronger explanation of the comparisons should determine the argument's weight. Scientific assessment requires specific research. The institutional demand here is that these questions receive timely, independent examination before the people raising them are excluded.

Weight belongs to particular claims. Work personally performed, contemporaneous material, and previously unknown details later verified strengthen the relevant account. Multiple layers of retelling, important contradictions, and concealed interests weaken it. Real professional and personal costs also matter when assessing motives. Protection should not wait until every detail is proved, while paying a price cannot ensure knowledge of an entire event.

An institution withholding originals cannot treat outsiders' lack of those records as sufficient proof of its own account. Under strict secrecy and censorship, failure to find public confirmation may carry little weight against a claim. Selective concealment of particular records that should exist can itself strengthen the grounds for inquiry. The Morens material gives reason to reassess the independence of the support concerned without awaiting an institutional declaration of untrustworthiness.

Those grounds must still connect to the question being answered. Protecting a reputation, concealing a mistake, and concealing an intentional crime can all produce secrecy. Distinguishing them requires comparison of timing, access, specific content, and subsequent conduct. Historical experience, economic dependence, and geopolitics help identify capacity, motive, and opportunity; they cannot supply the missing particulars of a secret act by themselves.

Complete opposition to the CCP does not certify independence, nor does agreement with one of its conclusions establish control. Similar views, voluntary accommodation, concessions under pressure, and obedience to instructions are different relationships. Conduct and indirect evidence can support judgments about them without an explicit written order. The material must explain a particular connection, rather than divide all sources into truth and falsehood by political alignment in advance.

If every unwelcome observation becomes further proof of an adversary's control, investigation loses its ability to discover its own errors. Where material is incomplete, readers can still identify the stronger explanation, state their confidence, and demand witness protection, record preservation, and continued inquiry. Truth does not emerge from a vote. Approaching it requires showing the premises and reasons behind each judgment.

## Who protects those who raise questions?

From Li's professional and administrative pressures to political direction in journalism, platform duties, scientific media contacts, official records, and hearings, this chapter has followed different gateways to information. Control can operate when information is created, circulated, preserved, or questioned.

Protecting publication without protecting those who discover problems may leave no alarm to publish. Allowing questions without supplying records may leave investigation dependent on conjecture. Releasing material without permitting independent interpretation may still impose a single conclusion. These capacities need to reinforce one another for knowledge to become usable power.

In its 2011 General Comment No. 34, the UN Human Rights Committee connected independent media with access to information held by public bodies, called for reasons and appeals when access is refused, and emphasized necessity and proportionality in restrictions on expression.[12](93-notes.md#ch10-t12) This standard places responsibility for justifying a restriction on the authority imposing it.

Ordinary people should be able to raise risks safely, obtain the basis of official decisions, challenge restrictions, and secure timely correction. Journalists and researchers need conditions for independent work; those disclosing wrongdoing need protection; holders of public records need duties to preserve and explain. Treating people as political participants requires giving them information with which to judge.

Protection, verification, and assignment of responsibility should not share a single threshold. Reasonable grounds should trigger receipt of material, preservation of records, and protection against retaliation, without requiring the source to establish an entire chain of command. Public warnings can distinguish observed anomalies from matters under examination; punishment requires evidence and procedures appropriate to the responsibility alleged. Binding these decisions together turns every day before the complete answer arrives into a reason to postpone action.

This book explicitly opposes the CCP's placement of Party leadership above journalistic scrutiny, which allows those being scrutinized to define its boundaries. Concealment within American institutions likewise requires exposure and accountability. A crucial institutional test is whether affected people have practical power to make records emerge, reasons face examination, and errors produce change.

The next chapter starts from established acts of information suppression and record concealment to concentrate on how the public can avoid similar harm. Dr. Li-Meng Yan's reports, Lude's disclosures, and subsequently released investigative material bring forward the question of how voices with little power can reach institutions, receive examination, and prompt action. Protecting people with knowledge, preserving records, responding promptly, and constraining retaliation need not await the end of every court proceeding. Institutions must make truthful warnings effective earlier while constraining fabrication and suppression.
