# Chapter Six · Open Markets and Political Continuity

*Privilege and Freedom: Who Makes the Rules, Who Pays the Price · English draft v0.3 · 17 September 2026*

## A promise about the children

In late 1978, farmers in Xiaogang, a village in Anhui's Fengyang County, made an agreement to divide farming among households. They promised to meet their delivery obligations and stop asking the state for money and grain. They also made provision for something else: if the cadres involved were imprisoned or suffered a worse punishment, the other villagers would raise their children to eighteen. The contract transcription supplied by the China Agricultural Museum preserves that promise.[1](93-notes.md#ch06-r01)

A document about production had to anticipate life without the parents who proposed it. The farmers wanted to reconnect their labor with their harvest. They also feared that changing how they farmed might cross a political boundary. Promising to deliver grain might not be enough to protect the people suggesting a different way to grow it.

The previous chapter examined how grain procurement and migration rules allowed the state to reach deeply into individual lives. The Xiaogang agreement raised a question in the opposite direction: what could be returned to a family's own judgment? Once a household could organize its farming, could it also require those making policy to listen to it?

The significance of reform began with such choices. Keeping more of a harvest or trying another livelihood could change people's circumstances and their understanding of themselves. People expected to await collective instructions were demonstrating an ability to judge and act.

This chapter examines how those choices expanded between 1978 and 1992, and why their expansion could coexist with continued political rule. Liberalism must recognize the opportunities individuals gained while asking whether the rights protecting those opportunities remain effective when those in power change their minds.

## From experiment to policy

The Xiaogang farmers faced two problems: how to increase their harvest and how to make a potentially effective method safe to adopt. Connecting labor to a household's return can encourage effort and improvements in farming. But while the method can still be declared a political error, another calculation restrains that incentive: can the extra grain compensate for the danger of losing one's freedom?

The communiqué of the Chinese Communist Party's Third Plenum in December 1978 criticized excessive concentration in economic management. It proposed greater operating autonomy for local authorities and enterprises under unified state planning. It also called for democracy to be established through stable institutions and laws that would not change with the leaders. Its agricultural provisions, however, retained the basic organization of the people's communes. The later spread of household contracting cannot simply be read backward into this meeting as an already completed plan.[2](93-notes.md#ch06-r02)

A change of direction at the center, permission for local experiments, and initiatives by farmers could reinforce one another. Farmers judged what was worth trying from their own circumstances, while political recognition changed the conditions under which experiments could endure. The relationship of power within reform was therefore concrete: households could propose methods, while those in power still decided which methods received protection.

In 1982, the central Party's Number One Document endorsed the *Minutes of the National Rural Work Conference*. The text expressly recognized forms of household contracting as production responsibility systems within the socialist collective economy. Arrangements that had been politically vulnerable now received affirmation in a central policy document.[3](93-notes.md#ch06-r03)

For a household, that was a practical change. It had a policy it could point out to an official, and the official had authority from above for permitting the arrangement. Recognition could thus reduce political risk for both farmers and local officials, giving more stable room to a choice previously undertaken in secret.

But recognizing an economic arrangement and allowing the public to replace those who made the highest policies were different changes. One could reduce control over daily life. The other concerned who possessed governing authority itself. Understanding reform requires following both.

## Giving improvements their place in history

Economic change cannot be treated as scenery. A joint 2022 report by the World Bank and China's State Council Development Research Center estimated that the number of people below the international extreme poverty line had fallen by close to 800 million over roughly four decades. The threshold used was $1.90 a day in 2011 purchasing power parity terms. This was a finding about several decades, not the 1980s alone. The report connected the transformation to agricultural productivity, industrial employment, urbanization, infrastructure, and poverty reduction policies, among other factors.[4](93-notes.md#ch06-r04)

Hunger and a lack of income can narrow people's attention to immediate survival. Escaping extreme poverty gives families more room to organize their lives, take risks, and imagine a future. The scale of poverty reduction deserves a place in history. Criticism of governing power must not erase ordinary people's labor and the improvements they gained.

For that reason, the question of political accountability must be put precisely. A family that benefits from a policy has not thereby surrendered all its future political rights as payment. Support for a policy does not amount to consent that its authors should remain beyond challenge indefinitely.

Production depends on workers, enterprise, knowledge, and cooperation. Government can create opportunities or obstruct them. Recognizing the value of policies that improve lives is compatible with requiring government to explain its mistakes.

Improving lives can also win support for those in power, making continued reform serve an interest in retaining rule. Benefit and authorization must be distinguished. An effective policy offers a reason to support it; it gives its authors no permanent right to govern its beneficiaries. Understanding economic results requires comparing particular measures. Establishing continuing consent to government requires people to be free to express and change their choices.

Economic reform therefore sharpens this book's question: once people gain more ways to earn a living, can they acquire the political capacity to protect those choices?

## Political conditions and limits on tenure

Rulers can want a more vigorous economy and a more orderly leadership at the same time. Either desire may encourage reform without making them willing to let the public replace the ruling organization. This distinction helps explain what particular rules constrain.

On 30 March 1979, Deng Xiaoping presented the Four Cardinal Principles as political conditions for modernization: the socialist road, the dictatorship of the proletariat, Communist Party leadership, and Marxism-Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought. Economic development could employ new methods. The Party's leading position belonged among the principles that had to be upheld.[5](93-notes.md#ch06-r05)

The previous chapter examined Deng's criticism of excessive concentration of power, patriarchal leadership, and lifelong tenure. Reducing personal arbitrariness and arranging transfers of office can also reduce conflict and uncertainty within an organization. Such limits have value. Their role in protecting organizational continuity still needs to be distinguished from their role in giving citizens the final choice.

The 1982 constitution did introduce identifiable limits. Article 79 restricted the state president and vice-president to two consecutive terms. Article 87 imposed the same limit on the premier, vice-premiers, and state councilors. The president's term and the State Council's term matched that of the National People's Congress: five years. The constitution also provided for freedoms including speech, publication, and assembly, and for criticism of state organs, appeals, and complaints.[6](93-notes.md#ch06-r06)

The state president, the Party general secretary, and the chairman of the Central Military Commission were different positions. These two-term provisions applied to the listed state offices, not to the general secretary. Article 93 gave the state Central Military Commission the same term as the National People's Congress, but did not impose a two-consecutive-term ceiling on its chairman. This distinction affects whether someone leaving one office can retain power through others.

A tenure rule can limit how long one person occupies a particular office. It does not by itself ensure that someone leaving that office relinquishes other sources of power. It also does not settle who may participate in choosing a successor.

Citizens making complaints encounter the same problem of power. If an investigator's prospects depend on the person being investigated, the political cost of proceeding may be considered first. For speech, appeals, and complaints to become usable rights, investigations must be able to reach the highest decision-makers, and those facing accountability must not control whether the evidence becomes public.

## A proposal to separate Party and government functions

When Party committees and government share overlapping duties, each can pass responsibility to the other while routine matters accumulate in political leaders' hands. Separating functions can improve administration and may give officials grounds to resist improper interference. Yet who chooses key personnel, and who can overturn a final decision, still governs that boundary.

Zhao Ziyang's report to the Thirteenth Party Congress in 1987 devoted a section to political reform. It proposed separating Party and government functions, requiring the Party to act within the constitution and law, and enabling state organs to perform their own duties. It also preserved Party leadership over political direction, major decisions, and recommendations for important state appointments, while explicitly rejecting alternation in government among political parties.[7](93-notes.md#ch06-r07)

Requiring the ruling Party to obey the law supplied a standard for public criticism. Reducing Party committees' control over administration could also make particular responsibilities easier to identify. These proposals had practical substance. But for the duties of state institutions to become a constraint, those institutions needed to be able to uphold the law when Party leaders demanded a different response.

But administrative separation remained distinct from political competition. State organs could receive greater authority to do their work while the organization determining basic political direction and recommending key personnel remained protected from replacement by another party. The public still faced the question of how the same ruling organization would reform itself.

A tension in organizational interests appears here. Leaders can need fewer bureaucratic obstacles, more local initiative, and more reliable succession while retaining authority to decide where reform ends. Reform can consequently advance and still be cut short when it reaches the ruling Party's position. If the highest leaders judge a demand to threaten Party rule, constraints must be able to force them to confront another judgment.

The hardest test of a reform often comes when the institution proposing it is challenged. Whether a division of responsibilities in ordinary administration can become a constraint during a political crisis determines whether people have gained better management or rights on which they can depend.

## People asking for an answer

The Hunger Strike Declaration of 13 May 1989 lets us hear how some protesters explained their actions. It raised rising prices, official profiteering, bureaucratic corruption, and concentrated power. It opposed the characterization of the student movement as turmoil and demanded substantive dialogue with the government on equal terms, together with recognition of the movement's legitimacy.[8](93-notes.md#ch06-r08)

The declaration expressed some protesters' demands, rather than a common platform for every participant. Particular allegations, including those about corruption, still require investigation. The demands themselves reveal a predicament: before obtaining an answer, the protesters had to establish their standing to ask questions without first being treated as people to be suppressed.

A dispute about representation lay behind the demand for dialogue. The state claimed to represent the people. Some of those people believed their problems were not receiving an honest response. They sought to be heard without first being classified as people acting in bad faith.

A hunger strike brings the body into political negotiation. One need not endorse every protest tactic to recognize the danger expressed in the declaration. When people cannot rely on a meaningful response through ordinary channels, they may conclude that only a greater personal cost will make those in authority listen.

The standard of accountability used in this book does not require protesters to be right about everything. Precisely because members of the public may be emotional, divided, or mistaken, government needs public reasons, clear procedures, and constrained coercive power. Disagreement with government should not remove a person's claim to legal protection.

## Gunfire in the streets, and a person's name

On 3–4 June 1989, troops advancing toward the city center along Beijing's western roads fired directly at citizens. A contemporaneous assessment by the United States Embassy in Beijing, dated 22 June, recorded these actions and casualties. Its account brought together embassy staff observations, other eyewitness reports, and secondhand information, requiring the basis of knowledge to be distinguished passage by passage.[9](93-notes.md#ch06-r09)

Deaths in the streets occurred in the spaces of everyday city life. Tiananmen Square became the event's name and symbol, but particular experiences still need to be located: evacuation from an area inside the square and shootings along the approaches were different courses of events. A family needs to establish where and how its relative was harmed, rather than whether that experience fits later arguments about the event's name.

Ding Zilin's son, Jiang Jielian, had just turned seventeen. According to her testimony, he was shot and killed near Muxidi late on 3 June. She did not witness the shooting; her account of it drew on a schoolmate and information obtained afterward. She experienced his departure and the wait for news herself. Her testimony also recorded the search by relatives and friends, and the death the family subsequently confronted.[10](93-notes.md#ch06-r10)

A name changes the scale of history. In political documents, officials describe a situation, an order, and its handling. For a boy just past his birthday, those decisions meant there would be no later life. For his family, the event did not end when traffic returned to the streets.

An investigation must establish firing positions, military units, orders, and individual actions. Incidents involving attacks on soldiers also require specific investigation. Particular crimes require particular evidence. Violence by some people cannot justify firing indiscriminately into a crowd.

A family's right to know why someone died does not depend on first establishing the nationwide death toll. Even one established case creates an obligation for the state to explain the facts, accept investigation, and answer for its actions. An incomplete total cannot be used to return a known death to silence.

## Whose test had the army passed?

On 9 June, Deng met senior officers of the forces enforcing martial law in the capital. He endorsed the military's action and interpreted the events as a challenge to Communist Party rule and socialism. In the same address, he insisted that reform and opening should continue, opposing a return to a closed country and a rigidly controlled economy. The defense of the existing political order and continued economic opening appeared together.[11](93-notes.md#ch06-r11)

Defining protest as a challenge to the system shifts the dispute from what government must answer to how the organization can preserve its rule. This was the political judgment of a leader supporting the action, rather than a full account of protesters' intentions. The hunger strike declaration preserves some participants' own demands.

This definition of a crisis can alter choices within the organization. If military obedience and political loyalty are treated as the key to surviving it, questioning the use of force may appear to weaken a common defense. Endorsing the action can instead consolidate ties between commanders and those carrying it out. This is an organizational mechanism inferred from the speech. Bereaved families need precisely an avenue of investigation free from that mutual protection.

Here was a sharp boundary to reform. Economic policies could be adjusted in response to results, and officials could face internal criticism. When Party rule was judged to be under threat, political coercion could be presented as a condition for preserving the reform project itself.

Liberalism must reject that equation: preserving a ruling organization's power is no justification for using lethal force against citizens. Government's duty to protect personal safety requires independent investigation of its coercive actions. Those making decisions cannot end families' questions by declaring their own actions correct.

## Choice in the succession

In a discussion with senior central officials on 16 June 1989, Deng called for support for a new leadership with Jiang Zemin at its core. He discussed earlier succession arrangements, expressed a wish to reduce his personal intervention, and warned of the danger in making the country's fate depend on the prestige of a few people. The Fourth Plenum, held on 23–24 June, removed Zhao Ziyang from the general secretaryship and other positions, and elected Jiang general secretary.[12](93-notes.md#ch06-r12)

Reducing personal intervention was a wish Deng expressed in the discussion. Specifying the leadership core others should support also showed him exercising influence over succession. The two can coexist in one leader: he may want a more stable transfer while believing that he must first ensure the right person takes over. Stability arranged through this logic depends first on agreement among the internal selectors.

The changes concerned the general secretaryship and other Party offices, rather than replacing the state president and the military commission chairman on the same day. Distinguishing offices allows us to trace when different powers moved and whether someone leaving a particular position retained influence over succession.

Within Party procedures, this was a reorganization of the leadership. From the standpoint of public authorization, the question remained unresolved. The appointments did not give ordinary people a mechanism for choosing between alternative ruling organizations. Someone opposing the response to the protests could not use the procedures described in the communiqué to require the Party to hand governing authority to another organization that person supported.

The departure of leaders, an organization's ability to arrange succession, and the public's ability peacefully to replace its rulers are separate capacities. The first two help an organization endure. The third determines whether its continuation must repeatedly obtain the consent of those it governs.

## Why markets could keep expanding

An organization seeking to remain in power may also need society to produce more wealth. If expanding production and exchange can increase income, employment, and support while the organization retains control of key political decisions, economic decentralization can become a means of preserving rule. Individuals can gain real opportunities while those in power use the gains to strengthen their position.

On 24 December 1990, Deng again argued against treating planning and markets as a simple dividing line between socialism and capitalism. In the same discussion, he upheld the people's democratic dictatorship and a united leadership core. He retained common prosperity as an objective while allowing room to choose economic instruments.[13](93-notes.md#ch06-r13)

The published excerpts of his southern talks in 1992 made the combination more explicit. Deng urged experimentation with markets and the use of foreign investment, treating the Party's retention of political power as an important condition. He also credited earlier reform achievements with helping the regime survive the crisis of 1989.[14](93-notes.md#ch06-r14)

These statements support an explanation in terms of interests: leaders can regard reform's achievements as a resource for surviving political crises. The same reasoning suggests that growth, employment, tax revenue, and technology can increase society's productive capacity while also strengthening the state and winning support for the existing order. Reform therefore has no inevitable, single path toward competition among political parties.

Households and businesses make their own choices among new opportunities. What they create can increase both their independence and the resources available to those in power. If licenses, interpretation of rules, and dispute resolution still depend on political connections, businesses may also regard maintaining those connections as a necessary cost of protecting their livelihoods. Expanding markets can thereby give existing power new channels of advantage.

Commercial choice nevertheless cannot settle the ultimate political questions. Who interprets the rules when economic activity encounters a demand from the highest political authorities? If someone gains an advantage through political connections, can those harmed investigate openly? If scrutiny reaches the leaders themselves, does it remain protected?

The answers help determine whether market wealth expands ordinary people's independence or also gives those controlling the rules new ways to gain advantage. To judge that relationship, we must follow particular powers, appointments, and interests, rather than simply count new enterprises.

## From choosing a livelihood to choosing a government

The Xiaogang agreement allowed farmers to imagine keeping more control over the fruits of their labor. Reform policies widened that possibility. Constitutional provisions and political reform proposals also held out standards: state institutions should follow rules, certain state leadership offices were subject to limits on consecutive terms, and the Party should act within the law.

The lethal violence of 1989 posed the hardest test of those promises. After members of the public made demands, the highest leaders defined a crisis, and the army acted, who could compel the decision-makers to answer the victims' families?

An assessment of reform must hold together the improvements that occurred and the questions of responsibility left unresolved. Lives can become markedly better while governing authority remains beyond public choice. Someone can value a policy and demand investigation of a crackdown; appreciate an economic opportunity and insist that their safety, and their children's safety, should not depend on a leader's political judgment.

To protect people, rights must remain effective when government does not want to answer. Institutions that benefit supporters must also allow opponents to speak safely, victims to obtain records, and those in power to face consequences supported by evidence.
