# A Note to Readers

*English draft v0.1 · 16 September 2026*

## Finding your way through the book

For a first reading, begin with Chapter One and continue through Chapter Twelve. The first three chapters establish the questions of inherited status, consent, and escape from domination. Chapters Four through Seven examine revolutionary organization, party-state power, reform, and succession. Chapters Eight and Nine turn to Hong Kong and interests across borders. The final three discuss information, warnings, and practical ways to constrain power.

Readers who want to start with contemporary questions may begin at Chapter Eight and return to the earlier historical chapters. The [glossary](91-glossary.md) explains unfamiliar terms, while the [chronology](90-chronology.md) helps establish the order of events. Its selected entries serve the argument; they do not constitute a complete history of China, the world, or the pandemic.

Numbered references in the text lead to the [notes collected by chapter at the end of the book](93-notes.md). Numbering starts again in each chapter, and the same material may be cited more than once. The notes also retain identifiers such as CH07-F09 to distinguish the chapter from the source group. References in the compiled electronic editions are directly navigable. External links in the notes lead to original documents or their public hosts.

## How material supports a judgment

The book distinguishes what a document says, what a participant reports, what occurred, and the explanations that may follow. A policy text establishes a rule or public position; implementation requires other records. An indictment establishes what prosecutors alleged, not a conviction. The value of testimony depends on the opportunity to know, its specific content, and the extent of verification.

These distinctions do not require equal weight for every source. Independent direct records, testable details, and reliable access can give one source greater weight. Repetition of the same report by several outlets does not automatically add independent evidence. Conflicts of interest deserve investigation; their presence does not remove the need to examine the particular material.

Analysis of political incentives, institutional consequences, and possible improvements often involves inference and normative argument. These need explicit premises and connections to relevant facts. An unknown element is not declared nonexistent because public archives are unavailable. Nor does the possibility of concealment automatically establish it.

## Version and references

The material cutoff for this reading edition is 16 September 2026. Chapters principally rely on material obtained and read as described in their source records. The cutoff does not mean that every external link was revisited that day, or that investigation of every relevant event has concluded. Reliable new material may warrant revision.

The Chinese edition uses Simplified Chinese. The English edition is written separately, preserving the same facts, degree of certainty, and source correspondence. Unless expressly identified as official, translations and paraphrases should not be treated as official translations. Narrative references use Dr. Li-Meng Yan or Dr. Yan; names within original document titles and direct quotations retain the source's wording.

The [annotated declaration](92-declaration-annotated.md) preserves the correspondence among the sixteen original claims while explaining the scope of verification, textual differences, and questions requiring further research. It is a reading commentary rather than a reproduction of the signatory list, and it does not treat the original text's assertive wording as an investigative finding.

Useful corrections identify the chapter, passage, and retrievable material involved, explaining whether the issue changes a fact, a translation, a premise, or a conclusion. Bringing disagreement to bear on something that can be examined is itself a contribution to free discussion.
